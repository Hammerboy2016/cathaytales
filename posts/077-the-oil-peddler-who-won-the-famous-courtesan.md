# The Oil Peddler Who Won the Most Famous Courtesan in Hangzhou / 卖油郎独占花魁

### *An oil peddler from Bianliang saved for an entire year to afford one night with the most desired woman in Hangzhou. He got nothing for it except the chance to hold her coat while she was sick. It was the best investment he ever made.*

*From **Stories to Awaken the World** (醒世恒言, c. 1627), Volume 3 — collected by Feng Menglong (冯梦龙, 1574–1646), set in the Southern Song of the 12th century · Translated and annotated by Cathay Tales*

---

> The year the Jin cavalry crossed the Yellow River, the city of Bianliang fell in a single afternoon. A girl named Yaoqin, then twelve years old, lost her parents in the exodus and was sold to a brothel in Hangzhou. By the time she was sixteen, the whole city called her the "Queen of Flowers." Men spent fortunes to sit in her presence. None of them mattered to her. The man who did was a peddler who carried two buckets of oil on a shoulder pole through every alley of West Lake.

---

## I. The Flight from Bianliang

When the Jin soldiers broke through the walls of Bianliang in the Jingkang era (1127), the refugees who fled south had no time to look back. Among them was a man named Qin Xiaolou, who ran a small grain and oil shop outside the city's west gate. He and his wife, both in their forties, had only one child — a boy named Qin Zhong, nicknamed Xiaomei.

The family got separated in the chaos. Qin Xiaolou and his wife were swept along with the imperial court to Hangzhou, where they arrived with nothing. They never found their son.

Qin Zhong, then thirteen, was picked up by another refugee — a kind oil merchant named Zhu Shilao, who had no children of his own. Zhu took the boy across the Yangtze to Hangzhou, raised him as his own, and taught him the oil trade. When Zhu died, Qin Zhong inherited the trade: two wooden buckets, a shoulder pole, a set of small copper measures, and a handful of regular customers. He was not yet twenty. He made barely enough to eat.

Meanwhile, on the other side of Hangzhou, a girl named Yaoqin had arrived under different circumstances.

## II. The Girl Who Became the Queen of Flowers

Yaoqin's father, a shopkeeper named Yao Bianliang, had also fled Bianliang with his wife and only daughter. But on the road south, his wife fell ill and died. A stranger named Boshan, who appeared to be a travelling merchant, offered to help the bereaved father and his twelve-year-old girl. He fed them, got them a boat, and took them as far as Hangzhou.

Boshan was not a merchant. He was a brothel keeper.

Once in Hangzhou, Boshan sold Yaoqin to a famous pleasure house run by a woman called Madam Wang Jiu. The girl was pretty, quick, and already literate. Madam Wang gave her the professional name Meiniang and put her through every refinement — poetry, chess, calligraphy, painting, music, dance, and the art of conversation. By fourteen, Meiniang was receiving guests. By sixteen, she was the most celebrated courtesan in the city.

People stopped calling her Meiniang. They called her **Huakui** — the Queen of Flowers. A single evening with her cost ten taels of silver, and even then, she only received men she chose. The wealthy sons of Hangzhou outbid one another for the privilege. She was, by every measure, unattainable.

She was also a prisoner. Madam Wang kept a tight hold on her, and Meiniang had long understood that her position was gilded but not free. She secretly hoped for a man worth marrying — someone who would take her away from the life — but among the fops and wastrels who came to drink her wine and quote her verses, she had not seen a single one she respected.

## III. The Peddler Catches One Glimpse

It happened by the purest accident.

One afternoon, Qin Zhong was delivering oil to a house near Zhaosuo Temple when a palanquin passed by on its way to a nearby garden. The curtain shifted. He saw a girl inside — not heavily made up, not smiling for anyone, just sitting with a distant, unreadable expression as if the noise of Hangzhou had nothing to do with her.

"Who is that?" he asked a bystander.

"That? That's Huakui Meiniang. The most expensive woman in the city. Even the officials' sons can't get in to see her without an introduction."

Qin Zhong did not say anything. He set down his buckets, picked them up again, and went on with his round. But from that afternoon, he could think of nothing else. He knew perfectly well how absurd it was. He was an oil peddler. He made, on a good day, two or three *fen* of silver profit. Ten taels was more than he could save in three years.

He did not eat well that night, or the next. In the end, he made a calculation. If he tightened his belt, put away every extra copper, and worked every day without rest, he might — in a year, perhaps a little more — save ten taels.

He decided to do it. Not because he thought anything would come of it. He simply wanted, once in his life, to be in the same room with her.

## IV. A Year of Buckets

For the next year, Qin Zhong did the following:

- He rose before dawn every morning and carried his oil buckets through the streets of Hangzhou in every kind of weather.
- He ate one meal a day, mostly rice and pickled vegetables.
- He patched his own clothes until there was more patch than cloth.
- Every time he earned a piece of silver, he took it to a money changer and had it melted into a small ingot, which he kept in a cloth pouch tied around his neck.

People in the trade noticed that the young oil seller was unusually diligent and unusually cheerful. He never argued about prices, never short-measured anyone, and never turned down a long delivery. The taverns and temples and brothels of Hangzhou all became his customers. His business quietly grew.

After a year, he untied the pouch and counted. He had **sixteen taels**.

Ten would be the fee. The rest he would need for proper clothes, a gift, and a little something for the servants. He went to a bathhouse, got his hair washed and re-tied, bought himself a clean plain silk robe, and presented himself at Madam Wang's door.

## V. At Madam Wang's Door

Madam Wang looked at him — a plainly dressed young man with calloused hands and no attendant — and assumed he had come to the wrong house.

"I've come to see Meiniang," Qin Zhong said politely.

Madam Wang laughed. "Do you know who she is? Young master, her company costs ten taels. Even then, she chooses her company. You — if you don't mind me saying — are in the oil trade."

"I know what I do for a living," Qin Zhong said. "I have ten taels."

He put the silver on the table.

Madam Wang stared at it, then at him. She had dealt with rich men who spent a hundred taels without blinking and then treated the girls like furniture. She had never seen an oil peddler walk in with a year of his life laid on the table in silver.

"Wait here," she said.

Meiniang, when told, was not impressed. "An oil peddler?" she said. "What would we talk about? Tell him I'm not available."

Madam Wang tried several times over the next month. Meiniang always had an excuse — a prior engagement, a headache, a poetry gathering. Qin Zhong never complained. He came back every few days, always polite, never pushy, and every time Madam Wang turned him away, he said, "Then I'll come another day."

Madam Wang, who had a hard head but a soft corner somewhere, began to feel sorry for him.

## VI. One Night

It was a winter evening. Meiniang had been drinking heavily at a banquet hosted by the son of a high official — a man she detested. She came home late, drunk and sullen, and announced she was going straight to bed.

Madam Wang saw her chance.

"Tonight is the night," she told Qin Zhong. "But let me warn you — she is in no mood to be sociable. If she is rude to you, do not take it to heart. If she passes out, let her sleep. You paid your money. You can at least sit by the fire."

Qin Zhong was shown into Meiniang's chamber. It was the most beautiful room he had ever seen — embroidered curtains, a bronze incense burner, a low table set with wine and porcelain. He sat down on a stool by the brazier and waited.

Meiniang stumbled in, saw a stranger sitting there, and frowned. "Who are you?"

"Qin Zhong. I sell oil."

She stared at him, then at Madam Wang, who gave her a look. Meiniang gave a bitter laugh. "So it's come to this. Fine."

She collapsed onto the bed. Within minutes, she was retching over the edge.

Qin Zhong did the only thing he could think of. He took off his own silk robe — the only good robe he owned — and held it out so she could be sick into it. He fetched her water. He wiped her forehead with a damp cloth. He sat with her while she drifted in and out of sleep, and when she woke at dawn with a headache and a dry mouth, he was still there, holding a cup of warm tea.

She looked at him for a long time.

"You're the oil seller," she said.

"Yes."

"How much did you spend to be here?"

"Ten taels. It took me a year."

She was quiet. "Why?"

"Because I saw you once," he said, "and I thought you looked very lonely."

## VII. What Happened After

Meiniang did not sleep again that morning. She talked to Qin Zhong — really talked, for the first time in years, to someone who did not want a poem or a performance or a piece of her. He told her about Bianliang, about losing his parents, about the oil merchant who had raised him. She told him about being sold, about the gilded cage, about the men who paid ten taels to look at her and never once asked her what she thought.

By the time the sun was fully up, she had made her decision.

She opened a chest, took out a bundle, and pressed it into his hands. It contained fifty taels of silver — money she had quietly put aside over the years without Madam Wang's knowledge.

"Take this," she said. "Go back to your life. Don't come here again. But remember this: I will not stay here forever. When I leave, I will come and find you."

Qin Zhong tried to refuse. She made him take it.

He left the brothel with the silver and a feeling he could not name. He went back to selling oil, but now he had capital. He opened a small shop. He bought better stock. Within a year, he was a modestly prosperous oil merchant.

## VIII. The Escape

Meiniang's chance came sooner than either of them expected.

A corrupt official named Wu Bazhi — a violent, repulsive man who had pursued her for months — arranged to have her abducted by boat and taken to his villa outside the city. He got drunk on the way and, when she fought him off, had his servants throw her onto a deserted stretch of riverbank and abandon her there.

It was the middle of the night. Meiniang had no shoes, no coat, no money. She sat by the water and wept.

And then, by an improbability that even Feng Menglong acknowledges is more than coincidence, a small boat came around the bend. In it was Qin Zhong, who had been out collecting an oil debt on the other side of the lake and was taking the night route home.

He saw a figure on the bank. He recognised her before she recognised him.

He wrapped her in his coat, took her into the boat, and brought her to the only safe place he could think of: a small house by the lake that he had recently rented for his business. There, over a bowl of hot congee, Meiniang told him everything.

"I have money," she said. "I have been putting it aside for years — enough to buy my freedom from Madam Wang, enough to start again. But I will not go back to that life. I will live in a room with whitewashed walls and cook my own meals, if it means I never have to smile at another man who has paid for the privilege. The question is: will you have me?"

Qin Zhong set down his bowl. "I have been yours," he said, "since the day the palanquin curtain moved."

## IX. The Reckoning

The next morning, Meiniang sent for Madam Wang and laid the situation out plainly: she would pay a generous sum for her freedom; if Madam Wang refused, she would go to the magistrate and testify about Wu Bazhi's abduction — which would drag the broadel's name through a court case that neither of them wanted.

Madam Wang, reading the room correctly, took the money.

Meiniang also settled accounts with every rival and false friend in the pleasure quarter who had ever mocked Qin Zhong or tried to keep them apart. She did it quietly, with a clear head and a full ledger — the same intelligence that had made her the Queen of Flowers now directed at building a different life.

Qin Zhong, for his part, made one more discovery. His real parents — from whom he had been separated on the road from Bianliang — were alive and living in poverty in a village outside Hangzhou. Meiniang, hearing this, lost no time in finding them, bringing them home, and setting them up in a room of their own. The old couple, who had grieved for their son for over a decade, found themselves with both a son and a daughter-in-law.

## X. What Feng Menglong Says

Feng Menglong closes the tale with a verse and a comment that is unusually direct for a huaben storyteller:

> The wealthy and the noble offered piles of gold and silk, and she would not have them. An oil peddler offered sincerity, and she gave him everything. Readers, do not think this is a story about a foolish courtesan. It is a story about how rare it is, in this world, for anyone to be seen.

The phrase that stuck in the Chinese literary memory is *独占花魁* — "the sole possession of the Queen of Flowers." But "possession" is the wrong word. Qin Zhong never possessed Meiniang. He simply saw her, once, through the gap in a palanquin curtain — and then spent a year proving that the seeing had been real.

She, in turn, saw him. That was the whole miracle.
