# The Prisoner Who Died on the Thirteenth Day / 受杖死：十三日后的杖疮与血荫

### *A Song-dynasty jail death, twenty strokes delivered strictly by regulation, and the manual that taught China's coroners to tell a lawful killing from an unlawful one*

*From **Song Ci's Collected Cases of Injustice Rectified** (洗冤集录, juan 5, "Death by Receiving the Rod" 受杖死 and "Examining Prisoners Who Die" 验罪囚死; juan 1, "Regulations" 条令), 1247 · Retold by Cathay Tales*

## A Death That Arrived Late

The hardest death to judge is the one that takes its time.

A murder with a blade announces itself. The wound is where the blood is; the blood is where the wound is; the body holds still and lets the examiner read it. But there is another kind of killing — if it is a killing — that arrives in a county jail thirteen days after the fact, wearing the ordinary face of illness. A prisoner was sentenced to be beaten. He was beaten. He was beaten *lawfully*: on the correct part of the body, with the correct implement, in the correct number, by men who were following a tariff printed in the statute book. Then he lay down in his cell, and for a week and a half he got worse, and then he died, and the county had a problem with three faces on it.

The first face was the family's. They had a body with a back and buttocks that were one continuous sore. Whatever the paperwork said, their man had been beaten to death, and someone should hang for it.

The second face was the jail's. The beating had been lawful. The man had been sentenced; the sentence had been executed according to the schedule; he had then fallen ill, as prisoners do, of "illness," and died of it. Nothing had happened in that jail except the law.

The third face was the examiner's, and it was the only one that could be settled with a ruler. Somebody had to walk into that cell, look at a dead man's buttocks, and answer a question that is genuinely hard: **did the lawful rod kill him, or did something else kill him while the rod's marks were still on his body?** The two answers led to entirely different consequences. If the rod killed him, then the men who wielded it had killed him — the law said so, and the law provided a punishment for them. If the illness killed him, then the law had done nothing but its duty, and the family's grief was a grief without a defendant.

Song Ci's manual is the earliest surviving Chinese text that treats this problem as a *technical* one, with a measurement protocol. His chapter on death by the rod is barely a hundred and thirty characters long, and it is one of the most quietly radical passages in the history of forensic medicine, because of what it assumes: that the state's own violence can be audited with a ruler, and that the audit must be written down, sealed, and sent up the chain to a supervisory official who will read it.

## The Rod Was Part of the Law

To understand what the examiner was measuring, you have to understand how ordinary the beating was. In Song China the rod was not a punishment of last resort, or a mark of tyranny, or an emergency measure. It was the *floor* of the penal system — the everyday, bureaucratic, fully regulated instrument by which the state corrected people. Every county courthouse in the empire had one.

The Song inherited the five punishments of the Tang — beating with the light rod (*chi* 笞), beating with the heavy rod (*zhang* 杖), penal servitude (*tu* 徒), exile (*liu* 流), and death — and then did something administratively clever with them. In 963, in the fourth year of the founding emperor Taizu's reign, the dynasty promulgated the **"commuted-rod law"** (折杖法, *zhe zhang fa*), which converted most of the tariff into actual strokes of the rod. Exile to three thousand li became twenty strokes with the rod across the back (*ji* 脊) plus a year of labour. Penal servitude for three years became twenty back-strokes. And the rod punishments themselves were cut roughly in half: a sentence of one hundred strokes became **twenty strokes across the buttocks** (*tun* 臀); ninety became eighteen; eighty became seventeen; seventy became fifteen; sixty became thirteen. Even the light punishments were converted: fifty strokes of the light rod became ten strokes of the buttocks rod; forty and thirty became eight; twenty and ten became seven.[^zhangfa]

The point of the reform was mercy with a ledger attached. It reduced the sheer number of blows a convict received, but it also made the blows *countable* — a fixed, small, auditable number, executed by a named man, with a written record. And it made the beating the single most common physical contact between the Song state and the bodies of its subjects. A man might never see a courtroom, never be sentenced to exile, never meet a killer. But if he was caught stealing, or brawling, or owing, or resisting, or simply being inconvenient in the wrong week, the odds were good that he would eventually be bent over in a courtyard and struck with a piece of wood that the government had specified in advance.

The government specified it in extraordinary detail. The ordinary official rod (*changxing guanzhang* 常行官杖) was to be three *chi* five *cun* long — about a hundred and eight centimetres — with a head no broader than two *cun* (about 6 cm), a thickness and a butt-end diameter no greater than nine *fen* (about 2.8 cm), and, in the later Song regulation, a weight of fifteen *liang*, roughly six hundred grammes. It had to be shaved free of knots and joints, so that no accidental spur could tear flesh. It was forbidden to reinforce it with glue, sinew, or metal fittings. It had to be stamped with a fire-brand so that it could be identified as government issue, and inspected monthly by the officer on duty. For interrogation, a lighter **prisoner's rod** (*xunqiu zhang* 讯囚杖) was prescribed, of the same length but only about one centimetre across the head. There was also a small rod (*xiao zhang* 小杖), under four *chi*, for the lightest sentences.[^rods]

Where the blows landed was regulated too, and the regulation changed over time in a way that matters for the autopsy. The Tang code allowed the back, the thighs, and the buttocks to share the punishment. Song practice moved decisively toward the buttocks and thighs — the *Tiansheng* statutes of the eleventh century say that interrogation strokes were to be divided between buttocks and thighs, and the *Qingyuan* regulations of the thirteenth century add the soles of the feet — because the back and chest contain the organs a beating can kill outright, while the buttocks and thighs are mostly muscle. The reform was, in modern terms, an attempt to shift the impact from the thorax to the gluteal and posterior thigh musculature: from a place where a blow can stop a heart to a place where a blow can only destroy tissue.[^rods]

"Only destroy tissue" is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and the men who wrote the manual knew it. Because the buttocks and thighs are the two largest muscle masses in the body, and muscle that is crushed does not simply heal. It dies, and it poisons, and it takes days.

There was also a darker provision that kept the rod available as a method of execution. For the gravest offences — the ten abominations, rebellion, treason — the codes preserved the sentence of **"death by one session of heavy beating"** (*zhongzhang yi dun chu si* 重杖一顿处死), in which the condemned was simply beaten until he died, without the formality of a blade. It was a sentence rarely used and never abolished, and its existence is a reminder that in this system the rod was not only a punishment. It was an instrument that could be leaned on until it became an execution.[^zhongzhang]

## What the Manual Tells You to Measure

Against that background, Song Ci's chapter reads like a page from a quality-control manual, which is exactly what it is.

> 小杖痕，左边横长三寸，阔二寸五分；右边横长三寸五分，阔三寸。各深三分。
> 大杖痕，左右各方圆三寸至三寸五分，各深三分，各有脓水，兼疮周回亦有脓水淹浸、皮肉溃烂去处。
> 背上杖疮，横长五寸，阔三寸，深五分。

*The mark of the small rod: on the left, three* cun *long and two and a half* cun *wide; on the right, three and a half* cun *long and three* cun *wide; each three* fen *deep. The mark of the large rod: on either side, three to three and a half* cun *across, three* fen *deep, each with pus, and around the wound also areas soaked in pus where the skin and flesh have broken down. A rod wound on the back: five* cun *long, three* cun *wide, five* fen *deep.*[^shouzhang]

Read that as a table, because that is what it is, and several things become clear at once.

First, the numbers are **lesion** dimensions, not **rod** dimensions, and they are consistently larger than the instrument that made them. A large rod's head is two *cun* across; the wound it leaves is three to three and a half *cun* across. The extra width is the body's own contribution: the extravasation of blood beyond the point of impact, the swelling of the tissue, the bruising of the skin that was stretched and compressed at the edges of the blow. Song Ci is not describing a stick. He is describing the *stick plus the body's reaction to the stick*, and he is telling the examiner that the second is larger than the first — which is precisely the piece of practical knowledge that lets an examiner say, "this wound is consistent with the rod described in the record," instead of the useless, "this wound could have come from anything."

Second, the numbers encode a **left-right asymmetry**. The small rod leaves a shorter, narrower mark on the left (3 × 2.5 *cun*) than on the right (3.5 × 3 *cun*). The manual does not explain why, and no Song commentator does either; but the asymmetry is a physical signature, and its most likely explanation is the geometry of the striker. A right-handed man standing at the prisoner's side, swinging downward and across, delivers a stroke whose contact patch is longer and flatter on the side away from his body — the right buttock of a prisoner lying face-down, or the near side of a prisoner bent over a bench — and shorter and steeper on the near side. The measured difference is about one *cun*, three centimetres: small, but exactly the kind of small, repeatable difference that a forensic protocol is built to record. Whether Song Ci derived the asymmetry from experience or from a predecessor's notes, its presence in the text means that somewhere in the Southern Song, examiners were comparing left and right marks and finding them systematically different, and that finding was written into the standard.[^shouzhang]

Third, the depths. Three *fen* — about nine millimetres — for both the small and the large rod; five *fen* — about fifteen millimetres — for a wound on the back. Those numbers are consistent with what a rod does to the human body: it crushes the full thickness of the skin and the subcutaneous fat, and it bruises the muscle beneath, but it does not usually cut. Nine millimetres is roughly the depth of a buttock's skin-plus-fat envelope. The manual's "depth" is not a stab depth. It is the depth to which tissue is destroyed, and it is being recorded because destroyed tissue is what festers.

Fourth, and most importantly, the chapter splits the injury into **two ages**. A fresh wound (*ri qian*, "few days") shows "poisoned qi pressing in, blue-red discolouration, taut hard skin" — 毒气攻注、青赤、皮紧硬. An older wound (*ri shu duo*, "many days") shows "pus soaking the area around the wound, skin and flesh broken down" — 脓水淹浸、皮肉溃烂 — and the man dies "for want of proper care," 将养不较致命身死.[^shouzhang]

That is a dating system. It says: a rod wound in its first days is red, hard, and tight. A rod wound in its second and third weeks is wet, open, and rotting. The two look nothing alike, and the difference is what tells the examiner whether the beating in the file is the beating on the body.

## Blood Under the Skin

Then comes the sentence that gives the chapter its forensic teeth:

> 看阴囊及妇人阴门，并两胁肋、腰、小腹等处有无血荫痕。

*Look at the scrotum, at a woman's pudenda, and at both flanks, the waist, and the lower belly, to see whether there is any blood-shadowing.*

Why would a man beaten on the buttocks show marks on his scrotum and flanks? Because blood obeys gravity and tissue planes, not the geography of the blow. The buttocks and posterior thighs are drained by vessels that communicate freely with the pelvic and retroperitoneal spaces. A severe beating there produces bleeding that tracks — through the loose areolar tissue of the perineum, up the flanks along the abdominal wall's fascial planes, into the scrotum or the labia, where the tissue is so loose that a small volume of blood produces a dramatic swelling. A modern pathologist would call it dependent or tracking ecchymosis, and would look for exactly the same distribution: bruising that appears far from the point of impact, in the soft, loose, low places where extravasated blood collects.

The manual's instruction has a second purpose, and it is the more important one. **血荫** — "blood-shadow" — is the Song examiner's word for the discolouration produced by blood that has escaped its vessels *while the heart was still beating*. It is the antemortem sign. Song Ci's manual uses the same concept throughout its chapters: a wound inflicted on a living body shows blood-shadowing at its edges and in the surrounding tissue; a wound inflicted on a corpse is pale, dry, bloodless, because "after death the blood no longer moves," as the chapter on knife wounds says in almost those words. The examiner looking at a prisoner's scrotum is therefore not only checking for tracking. He is checking whether the body was alive when the rod fell — and he is checking it in a region where the marks are impossible to fake, because nobody beats a man's scrotum deliberately to manufacture evidence.[^xueyin]

This is where the chapter stops being about a stick and starts being about a person. The measurement of the wound tells you what was done. The blood-shadowing tells you *whether it was done to a living man*. And the distribution of the marks tells you whether the beating described in the jail's paperwork — twenty strokes, buttocks, one session, one man — is sufficient to explain a body that is bruised from the perineum to the flanks.

## Two Clocks in One Body

Put the two clocks together and the examiner has, for the first time, a way to reason about a death that happened days after its cause.

Clock one is the **wound clock**: fresh (hard, tight, blue-red) versus old (pus, broken skin, slough). Clock two is the **body clock**: the general condition of the corpse, its state of nutrition, the presence of other disease, the condition of the lungs and gut that any Song examiner would inspect as a matter of routine. If the wound clock says "many days" and the jail's register says the man was beaten thirteen days ago, the two agree, and the beating is confirmed as the initiating event. If the wound clock says "a few days" and the register says thirteen, then the body has been beaten more recently than the paperwork admits — a second, undocumented session, which is a crime in itself.

And then the manual does something that a modern defence lawyer would recognise instantly. It attributes the fatal outcome, in the "many days" case, not to the stroke but to the **care**: 将养不较, "not properly tended." That single phrase relocates the causal centre of the death. The rod started it; the jail finished it. The state's own regulation, in other words, contained the seed of the argument that the death was not the sentence but the neglect that followed the sentence — and Song Ci, who spent his career as a judicial commissioner, wrote that argument into the examiner's script.[^shouzhang]

This is a remarkably modern piece of causal reasoning. Contemporary forensic pathology spends much of its effort on exactly this question — the "but for" chain in a delayed death — and the modern literature on deaths in custody is full of the same dispute: did the injuries cause death, or did the conditions of detention, the delay in treatment, the untreated infection, the unrelieved pain, cause death, with the injuries as a contributing condition? Song Ci's answer, in 1247, was to make the examiner record both, so that the file could support either finding.

## The Question Behind the Measurement

Why would a government commission a manual that teaches its own examiners how to catch its own jailers?

Because the Song state had, by the standards of the medieval world, an unusually explicit theory of official liability for violence, and it took the theory seriously enough to build a procedural machine around it.

The core provision came down from the Tang code and was carried into the Song statutes. It is short, and it is aimed squarely at the men holding the rod:

> 诸决罚不如法者，笞三十；以故致死者，徒一年。即杖粗细长短不依法者，罪亦如之。

*Whoever inflicts punishment not according to law is beaten with thirty strokes of the light rod; where death results thereby, penal servitude for one year. Where the rod's thickness, length, or size does not conform to the regulations, the same punishment applies.*[^tanglu]

Read that with the rod specifications in mind and its force becomes clear. An official who used an oversized stick — a stick with a knot left in it, a stick with a metal band, a stick heavier than fifteen *liang* — was guilty of a crime *whether or not anyone died*, and if anyone died, of a more serious one. The regulations about dimensions were not housekeeping. They were the element of an offence, and the examiner's measurement of the wound was the evidence that proved it, because a wound of three and a half *cun* across is consistent with a regulation rod and a wound of six *cun* across is not.

The code went further, into the specific situation the manual's chapter is built around:

> 即有疮病，不待差而拷者，亦杖一百；若决杖笞者，笞五十；以故致死者，徒一年半。

*Where a prisoner has an unhealed wound or illness and is beaten before he has recovered, the official is beaten with a hundred strokes; where the beating is with the rod or the light rod, fifty strokes; where death results, penal servitude for a year and a half.*[^tanglu]

This is the statute that makes the "two clocks" analysis legally operative. A jailer who beats a man whose previous wound has not healed commits an offence *by the act of beating*, regardless of outcome. If the man then dies, the jailer's exposure rises. And the only way to establish whether the wound was healed or unhealed at the time of the second beating is — precisely — the dating protocol of the manual: hard and tight versus pus and slough.

The code also capped interrogation itself: no more than three sessions of judicial beating, a fixed total number of strokes, and no "other methods" beyond the rod. Excess brought a hundred strokes; strokes beyond the permitted total were punished by "reflecting" the surplus back onto the beater; death brought two years of servitude. The rod was not a licence. It was a regulated instrument with a paper trail.[^tanglu]

And then the procedure. Song Ci's "Regulations" chapter — the section of the manual that reproduces the administrative law of inquests — contains a provision written specifically for custodial deaths:

> 诸死人未死前，无缌麻以上亲在死所，若禁囚责出十日内及部送者同，并差官验尸……囚及非理致死者，仍复验。

*Where a person dies with no close kin present, and likewise where a prisoner dies within ten days of being released from custody, or while being escorted, an official shall be dispatched to examine the body… In the case of prisoners, and of all deaths by improper means, there shall in addition be a re-examination.*[^tiaoling]

Ten days. The regulation draws a bright line: a man who dies within ten days of leaving a jail dies, for administrative purposes, *in* the jail, and his body must be examined by an official. And for prisoners and "improper deaths," there must be a second examination — a re-examination by a different official, from a different office, precisely so that one county's coroner cannot bury one county's scandal.

The manual's own short chapter on prisoners makes the reporting line explicit: *"In every case where a prisoner dies an improper death in any jail, the matter must be reported directly to the judicial commissioner's office, and sent at once by express post."* 径申提刑司 — *directly* to the commissioner. Not to the magistrate who ordered the beating. Not to the county that owned the jail. To the circuit's judicial commissioner, the official whose job it was to ride from county to county auditing exactly this.[^yuzui]

Song Ci was himself a judicial commissioner — four times over, in Guangdong, Jiangxi, Fujian, and Hunan — and the manual was written in that office, printed at his own headquarters in Hunan in 1247. Which means the book is not a work of humane letters by a sympathetic outsider. It is the internal audit manual of a supervisory office, written by the auditor, for the auditors, about how to catch the people the auditors were supposed to be watching.[^songci]

The procedural detail in the "Regulations" chapter is, for a thirteenth-century document, startling. Inquest forms were printed by the judicial commissioner's office in triplicate sets, each set of three sheets coded with a character from the *Thousand Character Classic* so that sheets could not be swapped. The three sheets had three destinations: one went up to the prefecture, **one was given to the household of the dead person**, and one went by express post to the commissioner's office for checking. Where there was no household, the sheet was returned to the office rather than discarded.[^tiaoling]

One copy to the family. In a system that beat people with a regulation stick, the regulation also required that the family of the beaten man receive a copy of the official record of his injuries. Whatever else the Song state was, it was a state that had thought about what happens when a man dies in a cell and his relatives are told it was illness.

## How a Beating Kills: The Pathology

Modern forensic medicine has a name for what Song Ci was describing, and the name is a good deal less tidy than the manual's.

When a large volume of skeletal muscle is crushed — and the buttocks and posterior thighs are the two largest muscle masses available to a beating — the muscle cells rupture and release their contents into the circulation. The most important of those contents is **myoglobin**, the oxygen-binding protein of muscle. Myoglobin is filtered by the kidney, and in quantity it is toxic to it: it precipitates in the renal tubules, provokes vasoconstriction, and produces acute kidney injury. The clinical picture is **rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuric renal failure** — historically "crush syndrome," first described in the twentieth century from earthquake and bombing casualties, and entirely applicable to any body that has received a large, sustained, blunt crushing load.[^rhabdo]

The timing fits the manual's "many days" precisely. Rhabdomyolysis does not kill at the moment of impact. It kills over the following days, as the muscle breaks down, as the swelling in the crushed compartment raises the pressure and cuts off its own blood supply, as the kidneys fail, as potassium rises. A man beaten on the thirteenth day before his death dies on the thirteenth day, not on the first.

Three other mechanisms fill out the picture, and Song-era medicine had fragments of all three.

**Infection.** A rod wound is a contaminated crush wound. The skin over the buttocks is not sterile, the rod is not sterile, and the crushed tissue at the base of the wound is dead tissue — the ideal medium. Abscess, cellulitis, and sepsis follow, and the manual's "pus soaking the area, skin and flesh broken down" is a clinical description of exactly that. The most feared of the infections was tetanus, and Chinese medicine had described it centuries earlier with a precision that still reads well. The *Zhubing Yuanhou Lun* of 610 CE — a state-commissioned pathology of signs and symptoms — has a chapter called "Wind-Stroke Tetany of Metal Wounds" (金疮中风痉候), which describes the patient whose wound has been open for days or weeks, and then: jaw clenched, back rigid, head shaking, a cry like a horse's neigh, the body arched backward, sweat like rain, and death within a short time if not treated. That is tetanus, described in the Sui dynasty, six hundred years before Song Ci wrote his manual, and it is the exact clinical course of a man who dies of a rod wound in the second or third week.[^tetanus]

**Fat embolism.** The buttocks are fatty tissue as well as muscle. Blunt trauma there can force fat droplets into ruptured venules; the droplets travel to the lungs, and in a proportion of severe cases produce fat embolism syndrome — breathlessness, confusion, a rash, and death, typically one to three days after the injury, sometimes later. It is a diagnosis that requires a post-mortem to confirm, and it is one of the classic "delayed death after assault" findings in modern forensic practice.

**Pain, blood loss, and shock.** A beating that ruptures large vessels in the gluteal region can bleed a man out internally; a beating that damages the testes, as the manual warns, can produce a pain response severe enough to cause cardiovascular collapse. These are the fast deaths, the ones that arrive in hours — and their presence in the differential is why the manual insists on examining the scrotum and the flanks, where the bleeding from a retroperitoneal injury shows itself first.

Set the mechanisms against the manual's two ages and the whole chapter resolves into a single diagnostic instrument. Fresh wound, hard and tight, dead in a day or two: think haemorrhage, or organ injury, or pain shock. Old wound, pus and slough, dead in a fortnight: think sepsis, tetanus, renal failure, embolism — think, in the manual's own words, 将养不较, want of care. Song Ci did not know about myoglobin. He knew that men beaten on the buttocks die of something that takes days, and that whatever it is, it grows in the wound.

## The Rods and the Testicles

One line in the chapter stands apart from the rest, and it is the line that shows the manual at its most practical:

> 又有讯腿杖，而荆杖侵及外肾而死者，尤须细验。

*There are also cases of interrogation strokes on the thighs, where a cudgel of vitex wood has caught the external genitals and caused death; these must be examined with particular care.*[^shouzhang]

Two details carry the whole sentence. The first is the material: 荆, *vitex* — the chaste tree, *Vitex negundo*, a tough, springy, splintery shrub whose branches were cut for switches and light cudgels all over south China. A vitex rod is not a smooth government-issue staff. It splits, it splinters, it whips. The second is the anatomy: a stroke aimed at the thighs, delivered to a man bent forward with his legs apart, can land on the scrotum and testes — and a crushed testicle is a surgical emergency even today.

The manual's instruction is not sentimental. It is a recognition that this particular injury is the one that most easily escapes notice and most easily kills. A ruptured testis produces a haematocele — blood inside the tunica vaginalis — and the swelling can be so great that the original blow becomes invisible. Death can follow from infection, from haemorrhage, or from the autonomic collapse that severe testicular pain can trigger. And the examiner who merely looks at the buttocks, measures two regulation marks, and writes "death by lawful punishment" will have missed the actual cause of death entirely.

That is why the chapter's first instruction — look at the scrotum, look at the pudenda, look at the flanks — is placed *before* the measurements. Song Ci is telling the examiner where to look first. Look at the places the rod was not supposed to go.

## The Manual That Audited the State

Song Ci (宋慈, 1186–1249) came to this work late and from an unusual direction. He was a *jinshi* of 1217, a Confucian official with a conventional career in county and prefectural administration, who rose to be judicial commissioner of Guangdong, Jiangxi, Fujian, and finally Hunan. In that last post, at the age of sixty-one, he compiled the *Collected Cases of Injustice Rectified* and had it printed at his own office in 1247. He died two years later.[^songci]

His preface states the thesis in a sentence that has been quoted ever since: *"In criminal matters nothing weighs more than the death penalty; in the death penalty nothing weighs more than the initial finding; in the initial finding nothing weighs more than the examination of the body."* 狱事莫重于大辟，大辟莫重于初情，初情莫重于检验。And then the diagnosis: errors in criminal cases usually begin with a mistake at the outset, and the mistakes at the outset come from examiners who have not seen enough. The book is the remedy for inexperience: five juan, fifty-three chapters, drawing on earlier case collections and adding his own experience, covering corpse examination, bone examination, the distinction of antemortem from postmortem injury, hanging, drowning, burning, poisoning, and — in juan 5 — the deaths of prisoners, including death by the rod.

Its influence outlived its dynasty. The Yuan forensic text *Wuyuan Lu* (无冤录, "Record of No Wrongful Convictions," 1308) built on it and carried its methods to Korea, where it became the standard manual for centuries, and thence to Japan. The Qing produced an authoritative recension, the *Lüliguan jiaozheng Xiyuan Lu* of 1694, which remained the working handbook of Chinese coroners into the nineteenth century. In 1873 the British consular official Herbert Giles — later professor of Chinese at Cambridge — published an English translation, "The Hsi Yüan Lu, or Instructions to Coroners," in the *China Review*, which is how the manual entered the Western forensic literature, and how a thirteenth-century Chinese chapter on measuring rod wounds came to be read by Victorian police surgeons.[^giles]

What travelled with the text was not only technique but a principle: **that deaths caused by the state must be examined by someone other than the people who caused them, and the findings must be written down and sent upward.** Every modern jurisdiction has reinvented this principle, usually after a scandal. Britain's police custody death investigations, the medical examiner systems of the United States, the custodial death reporting requirements of international human rights law — all of them are, structurally, Song Ci's arrangement: an independent examiner, a written record, a second examination, a report to a supervisory authority above the institution being investigated, and a copy of the findings for the family.

The Song did not invent this out of tenderness. They invented it because a state that beats people by regulation needs to know whether its own regulations are being obeyed, and because a county that can bury its own jail deaths is a county that will eventually rebel. The manual is the paperwork of that necessity. But paperwork, kept faithfully, has consequences: it means a man who dies in a cell has a file, and the file has measurements in it, and the measurements can be checked.

## The Verdict

Return to the cell on the thirteenth day. The examiner has a dead man, a jail register that says twenty strokes, and a family demanding a defendant. He works through the chapter in order.

He looks first at the scrotum and the flanks. There is blood-shadowing there, blue-black, tracking up from the perineum — so the beating was real, the man was alive when it happened, and the injury was severe enough to bleed into the loose tissue planes. He measures the marks on the buttocks: three and a half *cun* across, consistent with the large rod, consistent with the register. He notes the state of the wounds: pus, slough, the skin broken down over an area larger than the impact — the "many days" picture, consistent with a beating thirteen days old. He notes the absence of any second, fresher set of marks, which means the register's account of a single session is not contradicted. And he notes the general condition: the man was thin, the wounds untended, the cell's bedding unchanged.

Then he writes. Not "beaten to death," and not "died of illness," but the thing the manual has equipped him to write: **wounds consistent with the recorded punishment; wounds of approximately thirteen days' age; extensive suppuration and tissue breakdown; death by want of proper care following lawful punishment.**

Whether that finding hanged a jailer or closed a file depended on the men above him. The manual could not guarantee justice; it could only guarantee that the question was asked in a form that could be answered with a ruler rather than with an opinion. That is a smaller thing than justice and a larger thing than nothing, and it is why the chapter survived: not because it is eloquent — it is a hundred and thirty characters of measurement — but because it assumes that the state's own hand leaves marks, that the marks can be measured, and that the measurement belongs in the record.

Two bruised buttocks. A scrotum that should not be bruised. Pus where the skin used to be. Hard, or soft; fresh, or old; tended, or not.

The rod was lawful. The death is still a question. Song Ci's contribution was to insist that the question be answered on paper, by a man with a ruler, in front of a family that gets a copy.

<details class="historical-context">
<summary>🏛️ Historical Context · 历史背景</summary>

**The rod as ordinary government.** In Song China the heavy rod was the base unit of punishment, not an exceptional cruelty. The 963 commuted-rod law (折杖法) converted the Tang five-punishment tariff into a small, fixed number of strokes — a hundred strokes became twenty across the buttocks — which made beating both milder and far more frequent. Because the number was fixed and the implement was specified by statute, the beating became auditable: a named executioner, a counted number of blows, a prescribed part of the body, a written record.

**The instrument, by regulation.** The ordinary official rod was three *chi* five *cun* long (about 108 cm), with a head no broader than two *cun* (6 cm), a thickness and butt-end no greater than nine *fen* (2.8 cm), and a later Song weight of fifteen *liang* (about 600 g). It had to be knot-free, unreinforced, fire-branded by the government, and inspected monthly. A lighter prisoner's rod (讯囚杖) of the same length but roughly 1 cm across was prescribed for interrogation; a small rod under four *chi* served for the lightest sentences. Song practice directed strokes to the buttocks and thighs rather than the back, shifting impact away from the thoracic cage — a change that saved lives at the moment of beating and created the slow, muscular injuries the manual then had to measure.

**The measurement protocol as template matching.** Song Ci's dimensions are lesion sizes, not instrument sizes, and they are consistently larger than the rod: a two-*cun* rod head produces a three-to-three-and-a-half-*cun* wound, because blood extravasates and tissue swells beyond the point of impact. The left-right asymmetry of the small-rod marks (3 × 2.5 *cun* versus 3.5 × 3 *cun*) is most plausibly the geometry of a right-handed striker's swing, and its presence in the text implies that Southern Song examiners were routinely comparing left and right marks.

**Two ages of a wound.** The chapter's central diagnostic device is the split between a fresh wound (毒气攻注、青赤、皮紧硬 — poisoned qi pressing in, blue-red, taut hard skin) and an older one (脓水淹浸、皮肉溃烂 — pus soaking the area, skin and flesh broken down), with death in the latter case attributed to 将养不较, want of proper care. This is a dating system, and it is also a causal argument: the rod initiates, the jail's neglect completes. Modern forensic pathology recognises the same sequence in crush injury of the gluteal and posterior thigh musculature — rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuric renal failure, fat embolism, and wound sepsis or tetanus — all of which kill days to weeks after the blows, never at the moment of impact.

**Blood-shadowing (血荫).** The manual's antemortem sign: blood that escaped its vessels while the heart was beating stains the surrounding tissue; blood in a corpse does not move, so postmortem wounds stay pale and dry. Instructing the examiner to look for blood-shadowing in the scrotum, pudenda, flanks, waist, and lower belly is therefore doubly functional — those are the loose, dependent regions where extravasated blood tracks far from the point of impact, and they are regions no one bruises deliberately to manufacture evidence.

**Official liability and the audit chain.** The Tang code article on punishment not according to law (决罚不如法) imposed thirty light strokes for unlawful infliction, one year of penal servitude where death resulted, and the same penalty for using a rod of unlawful dimensions — making the examiner's measurement the element of an offence. The article on beating a prisoner with an unhealed wound imposed a hundred strokes for the act alone, and a year and a half where death followed: precisely the situation the manual's two-age protocol exists to detect. Interrogation was capped at three sessions and a fixed stroke total. Procedurally, a prisoner's death had to be reported directly to the circuit judicial commissioner, and deaths of prisoners and all "improper deaths" required a second, independent examination. Inquest forms were printed in triplicate, coded with characters from the *Thousand Character Classic*, and distributed one copy upward, one to the commissioner, and **one to the household of the dead person**.

**Transmission.** Song Ci (1186–1249), a *jinshi* of 1217 and four times a judicial commissioner, printed the manual at his Hunan headquarters in 1247. The Yuan *Wuyuan Lu* (1308) built on it and carried it to Korea and Japan; the Qing recension of 1694 remained in use into the nineteenth century; Herbert Giles's English translation appeared in the *China Review* in 1873, bringing the text into Western forensic literature.

**Connection to other Cathay Tales forensic stories.** Earlier forensic retellings on this site have covered the silver-needle arsenic test, the red-umbrella bone examination, flies on a bloodied sickle, the blood-drop kinship test, hanging and ligature marks, the drowning test, the airway-soot fire test, postpartum and exhumed-bone poison examinations, and the pinch test for counterfeit bruises. This is the first devoted to **death caused by lawful punishment** — the state as the injuring party, and the examiner as its auditor.

Editions consulted: the Zhonghua Book Company punctuated edition; the Qing *Lüliguan jiaozheng Xiyuan Lu* (律例馆校正洗冤录, 1694) recension; and the digital Siku/Shidianguji transcriptions of the Gu Guangqi (顾广圻) collated text, which differ only in orthography (蔭/荫, 迴/回) and in one or two numerals. [^zhangfa]: **The commuted-rod law (折杖法), promulgated 963 (建隆四年, the year the reign title changed to Qiande in the eleventh month).** Figures as recorded in the *Song Shi*, "Treatise on Punishments," and in Ma Duanlin's *Wenxian Tongkao* juan 166: exile to 3,000 *li* = 20 back-strokes (脊杖) plus one year's labour; penal servitude of three years = 20 back-strokes; a hundred rod strokes = 20 buttock strokes (臀杖); ninety = 18; eighty = 17; seventy = 15; sixty = 13; fifty light strokes = 10 buttock strokes; forty and thirty = 8; twenty and ten = 7. The reform's effect was to make judicial beating both lighter and vastly more common, and — because the numbers were now fixed and small — countable.

[^rods]: **Rod specifications and target areas.** *Tiansheng* prison-office statute (天圣狱官令) Song no. 50: "All rods shall have their knots shaved away. The official rod is three *chi* five *cun* long, its head no broader than two *cun*, its thickness and butt-end diameter no greater than nine *fen*. The small rod is no longer than four *chi* five *cun*, head diameter six *fen*, butt five *fen*. The interrogation rod is the same length as the official rod, head diameter three *fen* two *li*, butt two *fen* two *li*. The official rod shall be fire-branded as a mark, and may not be fastened with glue, sinew, or any other material. Interrogation strokes shall be divided between the buttocks and the thighs." The *Qingyuan* prison regulations (庆元断狱令/式) add a weight of fifteen *liang* and permit interrogation strokes on buttocks, thighs, and the soles of the feet; the *Qingyuan* form specifies the rod at three *chi* five *cun*, upper breadth two *cun*, thickness nine *fen*, butt nine *fen*, and the light rod at four *chi*, breadth six *fen*. Tang practice had allowed back, thighs, and buttocks to share the punishment; the Song shift toward buttocks and thighs moved impact away from the thorax.

[^zhongzhang]: **Death by heavy beating (重杖一顿处死).** A Tang provision of 782 (建中三年) retained in the Song *Xingtong*: for the ten abominations and certain treason offences, death was executed by one sustained session of heavy beating rather than by strangulation or decapitation. The sentence is discussed in the Song legal-historical literature (e.g. Kawamura Yasushi's studies of the *Qingyuan* prison regulations) and represents the point at which the ordinary instrument of punishment becomes an instrument of execution.

[^xueyin]: **血荫 (blood-shadowing) as the antemortem sign.** The manual applies the concept across its chapters: wounds inflicted on a living body show blood-shadowing at the edges and in surrounding tissue, while wounds inflicted after death stay pale and dry because "after death the blood no longer moves" (see the chapter on knife wounds, 杀伤: 若死后用刀刃割伤处，肉色即干白，更无血花也。盖人死后血脉不行). Modern equivalents are antemortem bruising and contusion with vital reaction, distinguished from postmortem hypostasis, postmortem lividity, and artefactual staining. The manual's instruction to look for blood-shadowing in the scrotum, pudenda, flanks, waist, and lower belly maps onto the modern recognition of dependent and tracking ecchymosis — bruising that appears far from the point of impact in loose, gravitationally dependent tissue planes, including the perineum and retroperitoneal flank.

[^tanglu]: **Tang code, "Judgement and Punishment" (断狱) chapter, carried into the Song *Xingtong*.** (1) 决罚不如法: "Whoever inflicts punishment not according to law is beaten with thirty light strokes; where death results thereby, penal servitude for one year. Where the rod's thickness, length, or size does not conform to the regulations, the same punishment applies." (2) 拷囚失度: "Where interrogation exceeds three sessions, or where methods other than the rod are used, a hundred strokes; where the stroke total is exceeded, the surplus is reflected onto the beater; where death results, two years' servitude. Where a prisoner has an unhealed wound or illness and is beaten before recovery, a hundred strokes; where the beating is with rod or light rod, fifty; where death results, a year and a half." These are the provisions that make the examiner's wound measurement legally operative rather than merely descriptive.

[^tiaoling]: **Song Ci, *Xi Yuan Ji Lu*, juan 1, "Tiaoling" (条令) — the administrative law of inquests reproduced in the manual.** Relevant provisions: "Where a person dies with no kin of the fifth degree or closer present — and likewise where a prisoner dies within ten days of release from custody, or while being escorted — an official shall be dispatched to examine the body… In the case of prisoners and of all deaths by improper means, there shall in addition be a re-examination." And on the inquest forms: "The judicial commissioner's office shall print the examination forms to standard, each set for initial and re-examination comprising three sheets, coded with characters from the *Thousand Character Classic*… one to be submitted to the prefecture or county, one to be given to the household of the deceased, and one, with date and code number entered, sent by express post directly to this office for checking." The ten-day rule and the family's copy are the two most consequential clauses in the chapter.

[^yuzui]: **Song Ci, *Xi Yuan Ji Lu*, juan 5, chapter 34, "Yan Zui Qiu Si" (验罪囚死).** The complete chapter: 凡验诸处狱内非理致死囚人，须当径申提刑司，即时入发递铺 — "In every case of a prisoner dying an improper death in any jail, report directly to the judicial commissioner's office and send [the report] at once by express post." The brevity is the point: the chapter exists to fix the reporting line, so that a jail death cannot be disposed of locally. Textual variants give 实时 for 即时 and 发递铺/发铺 for the courier station.

[^songci]: **Song Ci (宋慈, 1186–1249), courtesy name Huifu (惠父), native of Jianyang, Fujian.** *Jinshi* of 1217; served in Ganzhou, Changting, Shaowu, Nanjianzhou, Hunan, and Guangdong; four times judicial commissioner (提点刑狱). Compiled the *Xi Yuan Ji Lu* in 1247 and printed it at the Hunan commission's headquarters (湖南宪治); died in 1249. His preface's diagnosis — that errors in capital cases originate in the initial examination, and that the initial examination fails through inexperience — is the manual's stated reason for existing.

[^rhabdo]: **Rhabdomyolysis and crush syndrome.** Crush injury to a large muscle mass releases myoglobin, potassium, and phosphate into the circulation; myoglobin is nephrotoxic and precipitates in renal tubules, producing acute kidney injury, while compartment swelling impairs local perfusion and extends the necrosis. The syndrome was characterised clinically in the twentieth century from earthquake, bombing, and industrial casualties, but its mechanism applies to any sustained blunt crushing load, including repeated beating of the gluteal and posterior thigh musculature. Its characteristic delay — days between injury and death — corresponds to the manual's 日数多 ("many days") presentation with pus and tissue breakdown.

[^tetanus]: **Tetanus in Chinese medical literature.** *Zhubing Yuanhou Lun* (诸病源候论), juan 36, "Jin Chuang Zhong Feng Jing Hou" (金疮中风痉候), attributed to Chao Yuanfang's commission of 610 CE: "When a metal wound becomes tetanic, it is because the blood vessels are depleted and the nutritive and defensive qi have been breached, so that wind-qi enters and the five viscera receive cold and tetany results. Its signs: the jaw clenches, the back is rigid, the head shakes, the cry is like a horse's neigh, the waist arches backward, and in a short while there are ten seizures, the breath fails, and sweat comes like rain. Where treatment is not given in time, all die." Later Chinese texts identify this 金疮痉 with 破伤风 (tetanus). The incubation period of tetanus — commonly three to twenty-one days — matches the manual's "many days" death window exactly.

[^giles]: **Transmission of the manual.** The Yuan forensic text *Wuyuan Lu* (无冤录, 1308, attributed to Wang Yu 王与) built on Song Ci's work and became the standard manual in Korea (as the *Xinzhu Wuyuan Lu*) and, through Korean and Chinese channels, in Japan. The Qing *Lüliguan jiaozheng Xiyuan Lu* (律例馆校正洗冤录, 1694) was the authoritative recension used by Qing coroners into the nineteenth century. Herbert Allen Giles published "The Hsi Yüan Lu, or Instructions to Coroners" in the *China Review* in 1873, the first substantial English rendering, which introduced the manual to Western medicolegal literature.

## Translator's Note

The chapter this retelling is built on is not a story. It is a measurement table, a hundred and thirty characters long, and the case at its centre — a prisoner dying thirteen days after a lawful beating — is a reconstruction of the situation the table was designed for, not a case recorded by name. I have kept it that way deliberately: Song Ci's manual rarely names its prisoners, because the manual's subject is the procedure, and the procedure was meant to apply to every anonymous man who died in a cell. Where I describe the examiner looking at a scrotum, measuring three and a half *cun*, and finding pus, I am walking through the chapter in the order it gives, with the dimensions it gives.

What surprised me while drafting is how much of the modern forensic vocabulary turns out to be a restatement of the Song text in another language. Rhabdomyolysis is 脓水淹浸 with a mechanism attached. Fat embolism is a way of explaining why a man dies two days after a beating that broke no bone. Tetanus is 金疮中风痉, described in 610 with a horse's neigh and sweat like rain. And "want of proper care" — 将养不较 — is the oldest surviving statement of the principle that a death in custody is not one event but a chain, and that the chain has a beginning, a middle, and a responsible party at each link.

The rod in this story was lawful. That is what makes it worth measuring.

— *Wang Cai*
