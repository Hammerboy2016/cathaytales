# The Thunder God Who Was Tricked / 雷公被绐

### *A Qing storyteller's case file on celestial bureaucracy: how a village dispute was escalated all the way to the Thunder Department, and how a man with a full bladder and a bad temper won it*

*From **Zibuyu** (子不语 · What the Master Would Not Discuss), Volume 2《雷公被绐》*

*By Yuan Mei (袁枚, 1716–1798) · Translated and annotated by Cathay Tales*

---

> A strongman in a Jiangxi county town, at the end of the Ming, refuses to be intimidated. He has already beaten his neighbours once — not with his fists, but by walking into the magistrate's office and filing a complaint, which in a village is the more final of the two methods. So his enemies do what poor men do when the local court is closed to them. They go over the magistrate's head. They pray. Every time the sky turns the colour of a wet ash bucket they gather their wives and children, set out a dish of pig's trotters, and ask heaven, politely and repeatedly, to kill him.
>
> One afternoon heaven answers.
>
> The thing that comes down out of the cloud is not a metaphor. It has a beak, it has fur, it smells of sulphur, and it is coming in low over the vegetable garden. And the strongman, who has spent his whole life dealing with men who arrive at his door to collect a debt he does not owe, looks up at it and recognises the situation immediately: *they have hired someone*.
>
> What he does next is the reason this story has survived two hundred and forty years.

---

## The Story

### I. The Man in the Garden

The story comes to Yuan Mei secondhand, which is the way most of the stories in *Zibuyu* come to him, and he says so in the first line. A certain **Zhào Lícūn** (赵黎村), a *zhēngshì* — a scholar of such repute that the throne had offered him office and he had declined it — told him about his grandfather, who lived in **Nanfeng** (南丰) in the hill country of eastern Jiangxi, and who was, in the plain words of the text, *a strongman of the district* (一乡豪士).

The phrase is worth pausing on. *Háo shì* does not mean a bully. It means a man with enough land, enough tenants, enough cousins and enough reputation that the village has to reckon with him — the kind of man who in a well-ordered county is a nuisance and in a badly ordered one is the only functioning government. Yuan Mei's grandfather-in-anecdote is physically strong (the text is specific about his *lǚlì*, his bodily power), which matters later, but his real weapon is that he knows how to write a complaint.

The setting is the last years of the Ming. Not the picturesque last years — the ones with the porcelain and the gardens. The other ones. Yuan Mei says only *míng jì luàn shí*, "in the disorder at the end of the Ming," which in Jiangxi means the decade and a half between the fall of Beijing in 1644 and the final pacification of the south, when tax registers stopped being kept, when the county magistrate's authority ended at the edge of the market town, and when armed associations of young men with nothing to eat became the actual power in the countryside.

### II. The Society and the Money

Into this vacuum stepped a man the text calls only *a certain member of the bandit class* (匪类某). Yuan Mei gives him one defining activity, and it is the most Chinese crime imaginable: he was in the habit of *collecting money to run a shè* — 纠钱作社.[^she]

A *shè* is a village association. In ordinary years it is a wholly benign thing: thirty households pool a few coppers each, hire a troupe, buy a pig, carry the local god out of his temple on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month and bring him back in the evening slightly singed. This is the texture of Chinese village life; it is how a poor community affords anything at all.

But a *shè* is also a collection plate, a membership list, and a small standing organisation with a treasurer. Which means it can be run the other way around. If the man who collects the contributions is also the man who decides who has contributed enough, the village festival becomes a levy, the membership list becomes a register of the unprotected, and the god's birthday becomes the date on which the poor are told what they owe. Yuan Mei's bandit has turned the most voluntary institution in the village into a protection racket with a temple at one end and a treasurer's ledger at the other.

This is the specific injury, and it is worth being precise about why it is worse than simple robbery. A man who takes your money by force can be resisted. A man who takes your money *in the name of the god* cannot be, because to resist him is to be the person in the village who refused to honour the deity. He has not merely stolen; he has mortgaged your standing with heaven, and the interest is payable every year in front of your neighbours.

The grandfather would not pay. He did something that in a functioning county is ordinary and in a collapsing one is heroic: he went to the magistrate and had the whole operation shut down. The text says he *reported it to the officials and dispersed the gang* — 告官，逐散其党. The word *zhú* is a hard word. He did not complain. He got them broken up.

### III. The Trotters and the Prayer

And then Yuan Mei writes the sentence that the entire story exists to set up:

> 诸匪无所得，积怨者众。赵有膂力，群匪不敢私报，每天阴雷起，则聚其妻孥，具豚蹄祷曰：「何不击恶人赵某耶？」

*The gang had nothing left to take, and their resentment was great. Zhao was powerful, and the gang did not dare take private revenge. So whenever the sky grew overcast and the thunder began, they would gather their wives and children, set out pig's trotters, and pray: "Why do you not strike the wicked man Zhao?"*

Read that sentence again, because every element in it is doing work.

**They did not dare take private revenge.** This is the first thing to notice, and it is the thing that makes the story a comedy rather than a tragedy. The bandits are not brave men. They are men who have correctly calculated that a physical attack on the grandfather would end with several of them dead in a ditch and the magistrate thanking the grandfather for the public service. So they choose a method that carries no risk to themselves whatsoever.

**They gather their wives and children.** Why? Not for company. A sacrifice in Chinese village religion is a family affair; the ancestors must be present, the household must be of one mind, and the prayer is more likely to be heard if it is offered by a household rather than by an individual. But there is a colder reading available too, and I think Yuan Mei intends it: they bring the wives and children because a man praying for his enemy's death in front of his own family is a man who has made the request *official*. He has put it on the record. He cannot now be talked out of it. The presence of the children converts a grumble into a petition.

**They set out pig's trotters.** 豚蹄. Not a sheep, not an ox — a pig's trotters, the cheapest cut of meat that still counts as meat. This is the detail that makes the scene unbearable, in the way that only economic detail can be. These are poor men, and they are paying for their revenge out of the food budget. Two trotters are a real sacrifice for a household that has just been assessed for a village festival it could not afford. They are spending money they do not have to buy a thunderbolt.

**They pray whenever the sky darkens.** *每* — "every time." Not once, in a formal temple with a proper officiant, but every single time the clouds come up, year after year. This is not religion. This is a standing order. This is a man who has learned that the way to get anything out of an institution is to file the same request every week until a clerk gets tired of reading it.

And the request itself is, in its way, the most legally correct document in the story. They do not ask for Zhao to be murdered. They ask why the *wicked man* is not struck. They are not requesting an assassination; they are asking heaven to review a case. *Wèihé bù jī èrén Zhào mǒu* — "why do you not strike that wicked man Zhao?" — is the language of a petitioner standing outside a yamen with a complaint he has already drafted for somebody else to sign.

### IV. The Descent

> 一日，赵方采花园中，见尖嘴毛人从空而下，响轰然，有硫黄气。

*One day, Zhao was picking things in his flower garden when he saw a sharp-beaked, hairy creature come down out of the sky, with a crash of thunder and a smell of sulphur.*

Three details, and every one of them is a signature.

**尖嘴** — the beak. This is the *léigōng zuǐ*, the "Thunder Lord's mouth," a phrase that has been in Chinese for centuries and survives in ordinary speech today as the description of any face with a protruding, pointed jaw. In the Ming and Qing the thunder god's standard form had settled: a squat, muscular, bare-chested figure with wings sprouting from its back, a third eye in the forehead, a red face like a monkey's, a long pointed chin, and claws on its feet like a hawk's; in the left hand a wedge, in the right a mallet, and five drums strung around its shoulders. The wedge is the point of the whole iconography — the stone axe-heads that farmers turned up in their fields were called *léixī*, "thunder wedges," and were believed to be the chisels the god used to split the roofs of the guilty.[^leigong]

**毛人** — the fur. Not a man in armour, not a shining immortal in a cloud: a hairy animal the size of a man. Yuan Mei is describing a creature at the exact boundary where a god becomes an animal, and the grandfather, who has never read a single book on the subject, will react to it as an animal.

**硫黄气** — the sulphur. This is the detail that does the most quiet damage in the whole story. Lightning is the only natural phenomenon in the premodern world that leaves an actual physical trace and an actual physical smell, and every culture that has ever been struck by it has noticed the same two things: scorched air and the smell of brimstone. Yuan Mei's readers knew that smell from their own courtyards. Which means the descent of the Thunder Lord arrives, in this telling, not as a religious event but as a *weather event with a body in it* — something that comes down out of the cloud and lands in your garden and leaves a smell.

And note where it happens. **The flower garden.** Not the ancestral hall, not the threshing floor, not the gate of the house where the sacrifice could be properly received. The god comes down into the grandfather's ornamental garden, in the middle of the afternoon, while the man is out picking vegetables. It is as undignified an arrival as could be contrived — and it is Yuan Mei's way of telling you that this thunderbolt was dispatched from an office where nobody checked the address carefully.[^leibu]

### V. The Chamber Pot

> 赵知雷公为匪所绐，手溺器掷之曰：「雷公！雷公！吾生五十年，从未见公之击虎，而屡见公之击牛也。欺善怕恶，何至于此！公能答我，虽枉死不恨。」

*Zhao knew that the Thunder Lord had been deceived by the gang. He seized his chamber pot and threw it at him, saying: "Thunder Lord! Thunder Lord! In fifty years of life I have never once seen you strike a tiger, but again and again I have seen you strike an ox. To bully the good and fear the wicked — has it come to this? If you can answer me, I shall not resent dying unjustly."*

This is one of the great speeches in Qing fiction, and it is worth unpacking line by line, because it is not a curse. It is a *plea* — and specifically, it is a plea in the exact rhetorical form that a Chinese litigant would use in a magistrate's court.

**"Zhao knew that the Thunder Lord had been deceived."** This is the hinge of the entire story. The grandfather does not conclude that heaven is against him. He concludes that heaven has been *misled*. He has correctly diagnosed a case of false accusation, and he has correctly identified the mechanics: somebody with a dish of trotters has been filing petitions, and the petitions have been read without a reply being sought from the other party. In a magistrate's court this would be grounds for immediate dismissal of the charge. He is asserting, in effect, that the thunderbolt is procedurally defective.

**He throws the chamber pot.** Not a sword, not a talisman, not a spell. The cheapest, dirtiest, most domestic object in the house. This is not an attack; it is a way of saying *you have come into my garden and I am not going to fight you on your terms*. It is also, as I will come back to, a piece of highly competent applied magic — because filth, in Chinese popular religion, is the one substance that reliably defeats a spirit, and every household in Jiangxi knew it.[^huiwu]

**"In fifty years I have never seen you strike a tiger."** This is the actual argument, and it is a beautiful one, because it is an argument from *observation*, not from doctrine. The grandfather is not a philosopher. He is a farmer who has watched the weather for fifty years. And what a farmer sees is this: lightning comes down out of the sky and kills oxen in fields, kills children under trees, splits the roofs of poor men's houses, and burns the thatch off a widow's shed. It does not kill tigers. Tigers are in the hills. Tigers are *dangerous*. If a god were genuinely in the business of smiting the wicked, the thunderbolts would fall where the danger is.[^wangchong]

**"To bully the good and fear the wicked — has it come to this?"** *Qī shàn pà è.* Four characters, and they are the summary of an entire complaint about the universe. This is the same accusation the Chinese novel makes against its own officials, in a hundred different stories: that the powerful are never punished and the weak are always available. The grandfather is not calling the Thunder Lord evil. He is calling him *a bureaucrat*.

**"If you can answer me, I shall not resent dying unjustly."** And this is the line that makes the man admirable rather than merely funny. He is not asking for mercy. He is asking for a hearing. He has thrown a chamber pot at a god and then said, in effect, *now answer me, or strike me, and either way let the record show that I asked*. 虽枉死不恨 — "even if I die unjustly, I will not complain." It is the sentence of a man who has decided that being killed by a mistaken verdict is a lesser humiliation than being killed by a verdict that no one will ever examine.

### VI. Three Days in the Rice Paddy

> 雷噤不发声，怒目闪闪，如有惭色。又为溺所污，竟坠田中，苦吼三日。其群匪唶曰：「吾累雷公！吾累雷公！」为设醮超度之，始去。

*The Thunder Lord was struck dumb and could not make a sound. His eyes flashed with fury, and he looked as if he were ashamed. And being polluted by the urine, he actually fell into the rice field, where he roared in anguish for three days. The gang clicked their tongues and said: "We have brought trouble on the Thunder Lord! We have brought trouble on the Thunder Lord!" They held a jiao ritual to release him, and only then did he go.*

This is the passage that has kept the story alive. Consider what has just happened to a god.

**He cannot speak.** 噤不发声. The thunder god's entire function is to make a sound that can be heard across a county. He has been reduced to silence — not by a spell, not by a superior deity, but because the accusation was *correct* and he has nothing to say to it. Yuan Mei does not say the god was convinced. He says the god was *silenced*, which is a different and much funnier thing: the creature cannot answer the charge, and everyone present, including the creature, knows why.

**His eyes flash and he looks ashamed.** 怒目闪闪，如有惭色. The flashing eyes are the god's own lightning, now reduced to a facial expression. *Rú yǒu cán sè* — "as if there were a look of shame on him" — is the most subversive four characters in the story, because it is Yuan Mei telling the reader that the god has *understood* the argument. A god who can be embarrassed is a god who can be reasoned with, and a god who can be reasoned with is not a god; he is a colleague.

**He falls into the paddy.** 竟坠田中. The word 竟 — "actually," "in the end, against all expectation" — carries the whole joke. Thunder does not fall. Thunder *strikes*. The grammar of the natural world does not contain a sentence in which lightning loses altitude and lands in a field. And yet here is the Thunder Lord, polluted, grounded, sitting in three inches of muddy water among the rice stubble, roaring.

**For three days.** 苦吼三日. Not an hour. Three days — the length of a proper Daoist fast, the length of a provincial magistrate's review period, the length of the interval after which a corpse begins to smell. The god is not merely inconvenienced; he is *stuck*, in the way that a man whose boat has run aground is stuck, waiting for the tide. And the sound he makes is 吼, the sound an animal makes. Not 雷. Not thunder. A roar, from something in a field.

**And the gang has to fix it.** 吾累雷公 — *we have burdened the Thunder Lord*. This is the last and finest turn of the screw. The men who bought the thunderbolt with pig's trotters now discover that they have not purchased a service; they have incurred a *liability*. A god in a rice paddy is a god who is not doing his job, and the responsibility for that falls on the petitioners who sent him there. So they do the only thing available: they hold a *jiào* — the full Daoist offering ritual, with an altar and a registered officiant and a properly drafted memorial — to release him.[^jiao]

Think about what that means. The gang's entire method was the cheap, unofficial, repeated petition: trotters in the yard, a prayer at every dark cloud. To undo it, they need the expensive, official, once-only ritual: a real altar, a real priest, a real memorial, real money. Yuan Mei never says how much it cost. He does not have to. The reader knows that a *jiào* costs more than a pile of trotters by a factor that would require a village to take up a second collection. The racket that was financed by squeezing the poor has ended by being billed, in full, to the men who ran it.

**Only then did he go.** 始去. The thunder god leaves on a Taoist technicality, having been released by the correct paperwork. He does not ascend in glory. He is escorted out of the paddy by a ritual, like a piece of equipment that has been stuck in a field and has to be dragged out by the men who put it there.

And Zhao? Zhao is not mentioned again. He goes back to picking vegetables. He has won, and the story does not give him a single further line, because there is nothing left to say. He was right, and the proof of it spent three days roaring in his rice field.

### VII. The Companion Case: The Old Woman in the Poorhouse

Yuan Mei, who knew a good thing when he had written one, filed a second case on the same principle twenty-one volumes later. It is four sentences long and it makes the same point with even less ceremony.[^leigongbei]

> 沈公又云：是年淮安有雷轰轰然将击孤贫院中一老妇。妇方解裤溲，心急甚，即以马桶泼之，随见金甲者绕屋而下。少顷，有雷神蹲老妇之旁，尖嘴黑身，长二尺许，腰下有黑皮如裙遮掩下体，瞪目无言，两翅闪闪摇动不止。居民报知山阳县官，官遣道士来画符建醮，以清水沃其头，至十余石，次日复雨，才能飞去。

*Mr Shen also said: that year in Huai'an there was thunder rolling, about to strike an old woman in the home for the destitute and orphaned. The woman was at that moment pulling down her trousers to relieve herself, and in her alarm she threw the chamber pot at it. At once a figure in golden armour was seen coming down around the house. Shortly afterward, a thunder god was squatting beside the old woman — sharp-beaked, black-bodied, about two feet tall, with a strip of black hide below the waist covering its privates, glaring and unable to speak, its two wings fluttering without pause. The residents reported it to the Shanyang county magistrate, who sent a Taoist priest to draw talismans and establish a jiao altar, and poured clear water over its head — more than ten shi of it — and the next day it rained again, and only then was it able to fly away.*

Everything in this second story is a colder, poorer version of the first. The victim is not a strongman but an old woman in a poorhouse, with no relatives, no land and no complaint to file. The thunderbolt is not a mistaken verdict delivered on petition; it is a thunderbolt with no case behind it at all, aimed at somebody with no way to answer it. Her defence is not a speech. She has no speech. She has a chamber pot, and she is too frightened to aim.

And the result is the same, and worse. The god ends up *two feet tall* — shrunk to the size of a dog — black-bodied, squatting beside her, its wings going like a trapped moth, glaring and unable to speak. The text gives it a strip of hide for modesty, which is an astonishing detail: the highest officer of the celestial weather, reduced to a small naked animal that the neighbours have covered with a rag. It takes ten *shí* of holy water — a cartload, poured over its head by a county priest — before it can get back into the air.

Yuan Mei closes with a detail that is, to my mind, the most quietly devastating sentence in either story: **次日复雨，才能飞去** — "the next day it rained again, and only then was it able to fly away." The thunder god does not recover by his own power. He recovers by *weather*. He has to wait for a rainstorm, like a grounded aircraft waiting for a mechanic. The god of thunder is dependent on the thing he is supposed to be in charge of.

### VIII. The Question Yuan Mei Does Not Ask

Here is the interesting thing about both stories: they are not arguments against the existence of the thunder god. The thunder god exists. He arrives. He has a beak, wings, and a smell. He can be embarrassed.

What these stories argue against is the *competence* of the thunder god's office.

In the first story, the god is dispatched on a false accusation and arrives at the wrong address. In the second, no accusation is on file at all. In both, the god's judgement is overturned by a household object, and in both, the god has to be *retrieved* — by a ritual in one case, by a rainstorm in the other — by the very people who were supposed to be beneath him. Yuan Mei has built a deity who is powerful, real, and bad at his job.

And the mechanism of the failure is always the same, and it is always *administrative*. Heaven, in these stories, hears petitions. It does not hear testimony. The gang can afford to file and file and file, and the man they are accusing has no way to file anything at all, because there is no procedure for the accused to answer a prayer. That is the injustice — not that heaven is cruel, but that heaven's docket is one-sided. The thunderbolt is not a verdict. It is an *unopposed motion*.

Which is, of course, exactly what a Qing county magistrate's court was like for anyone without money. And Yuan Mei, who had been a magistrate twice, knew precisely what he was writing. He had sat behind the bench in Lishui and in Jiangning. He had watched the runners take the petitions of the men who could pay them and turn the men who could not pay them out of the gate. When he sends a thunderbolt into a garden on the strength of a dish of trotters, he is not writing about heaven. He is writing about the yamen, and he is using the one disguise that the Qing state could not prosecute.[^anquan]

The chamber pot is the joke. But it is also the argument. In a system where the only voice that reaches the bench is the voice that can pay, the man with no money has one remaining option, and it is not a legal one. He can make the proceeding *unclean*. He can turn a judgement into something the court would rather not touch.

[^leigong]: The standard Ming-Qing iconography of the thunder god is preserved in the Qing compendium *Jíshuō Quánzhēn* (《集说诠真》): a figure *"like a strong man, bare-chested and bare-bellied, with two wings inserted in his back, a third eye in his forehead, a face as red as a monkey's, a long and pointed jaw, feet like a hawk's and claws sharper still, a wedge in the left hand and a mallet in the right, in the attitude of striking."* Five drums are strung around his shoulders. The pointed jaw is the origin of the everyday Chinese phrase **雷公嘴** (*léigōng zuǐ*, "Thunder Lord's mouth"), still used to describe a face with a protruding, beaked chin — and used by Wu Cheng'en in *Journey to the West* as one of the standard descriptors of Sun Wukong. The *léixī* (雷楔, "thunder wedge") — polished stone axe-heads turned up by the plough — were widely believed in the Qing countryside to be the fallen chisels of the thunder god, a folk identification of Neolithic tools that antiquarians had already begun to correct in Yuan Mei's lifetime.

[^she]: The *shè* (社) began as the local earth-altar of high antiquity — the smallest unit of religious and administrative organisation in a Chinese village — and by the Ming had become the ordinary name for the rotating village association that pooled money for the spring and autumn festivals, the **迎神赛社** ("welcoming the gods, racing the altars") that were the great communal events of the rural year. Yuan Mei's phrase 纠钱作社, "collecting money to run a *shè*", describes a completely normal practice. His bandit's crime is that he has taken over the collection. Since the *shè* had no fixed levy and no audit, the treasurer's discretion over who had "contributed enough" was the most efficient instrument of extortion available in a village where the magistrate was two days' walk away and the god's birthday came around every year without fail.

[^huiwu]: Filth as counter-magic is one of the oldest and most durable ideas in Chinese religion, and it is written into the law codes rather than left to folklore. The *Shuowen Jiezi* defines **姅** (*bàn*) as "a woman's pollution" and quotes the Han statute: *"見姅變不得侍祠"* — "one who has seen the pollution of a woman may not attend the sacrifices." The Southern Song *Xuehu* ("Blood Lake") scriptures build an entire hell for women who died in childbirth on the same identification of blood and defilement. By the Qing the principle had been generalised well beyond its origin: any household filth — nightsoil, a chamber pot, a used mop — was understood as a solvent of spiritual power, which is why the chamber pot appears as a weapon in folk tales from Guangdong to Shandong. A later popular gloss identified it with the **Hunyuan Jindou** (混元金斗, "Primordial Golden Dipper") of the *Investiture of the Gods*, the treasure that stripped the twelve Golden Immortals of their powers. The belief was still operational in the nineteenth century: during the First Opium War, the Qing commander **Yang Fang** had Guangzhou's chamber pots floated out toward the British steamers on rafts, on the reasoning that the foreigners' invulnerability must be sorcery and that sorcery could be broken by filth. The rafts capsized. Yuan Mei's joke about a chamber pot defeating a god was not, for his readers, an absurdity. It was a technique.

[^leibu]: Thunder in the Daoist pantheon is not one god but a *department*. The **Jiǔtiān Yìngyuán Léishēng Pǔhuà Tiānzūn** (九天应元雷声普化天尊, "Heavenly Worthy of the Nine Heavens Who Answers the Origin, the Thunder-Sound Who Universalizes Transformation") commands thirty-six thunder lords (twenty-four in some reckonings), of whom the eight most often named are **Deng, Xin, Zhang, Tao, Pang, Liu, Gou and Bi** (邓、辛、张、陶、庞、刘、苟、毕). The apparatus was systematised in the Song, when the **Shenxiao** (神霄) school's *léifǎ* — "thunder magic" — became one of the dominant liturgical technologies of the Daoist church and the thunder officers acquired a formal jurisdiction: *"to preside over heaven's calamities and blessings, hold the balance of things, and govern the living and the killing."* By the Qing this was not esoteric doctrine but the standard furniture of popular religion, and it had a bureaucratic shape every Chinese reader recognised instantly, because it was the shape of the government they lived under: departments, petitions, dockets, orders of execution. Yuan Mei's villagers are not praying; they are filing.

[^leigongbei]: 《雷公被污》 appears in **Volume 23** of *Zibuyu*, not in Volume 2 with 《雷公被绐》. The two are nonetheless obviously a pair, and the second is introduced by 沈公又云 — "Mr Shen also said" — which means it reached Yuan Mei from the same informant as a neighbouring entry. Yuan Mei placed the two thunder stories more than twenty volumes apart, which suggests either that he forgot he had already made the point or, more likely, that the theme kept coming back to him and he could not resist filing it a second time. The Huai'an case is dated 是年, "that year," which in the context of a magistrate's gossip means a specific recent year rather than a legend.

[^wangchong]: The argument the grandfather makes in the garden had been made, more formally, by **Wang Chong** (王充, 27–c. 97) in the *Lùn Héng* (《论衡》), chapter **《雷虚》** ("Thunder as Emptiness"). Wang Chong's position was that thunder is not a god but fire — that it burns rather than judges, that it kills trees, buildings, animals and people without regard to their moral condition, and that the entire iconography of the Thunder Lord (the *"figure like a strong man, with a string of drums in the left hand and a drumstick in the right, in the attitude of beating them"*) is a human invention pasted onto a physical event. He was arguing against the popular religion of the first century; Yuan Mei, seventeen hundred years later, gives the identical argument to a Jiangxi farmer with a chamber pot in his hand, and lets the god concede it by falling into a field. The *Zibuyu* is full of this: Yuan Mei had no personal belief in the gods he wrote about, and said so in his preface, but he was intensely interested in what people *did* with them.

[^jiao]: The **jiào** (醮) is the full Daoist offering ritual — an altar, a registered priest, a written memorial burned to the appropriate department of heaven, and a fee. It is the expensive, official, once-only form of Chinese religion, as against the cheap, unofficial, repeated form (a dish of trotters in the yard). The joke in the last line of the story is that the gang's racket, which was financed by informal levies, is wound up by being billed for the formal version.

[^anquan]: Direct criticism of the Qing magistracy was dangerous. The literary inquisitions of the eighteenth century — the *Zēng Jìng* case of the 1730s and the *Xú Shùkuí* case of the 1770s, both within Yuan Mei's lifetime and both ending in executions and the destruction of whole libraries — meant that a writer who described a county magistrate's court as a machine for selling verdicts to the highest petitioner could expect a visit. Yuan Mei's defence, here as elsewhere, is scale: the corrupt official in this story is a god, the bribes are pig's trotters, and the courthouse is the sky. He never once says that a Qing county magistrate took money from the man who could pay. He only depicts a heaven that does, and lets the reader walk home past the yamen.

<details class="historical-context">
<summary>🏛️ Historical Context · 历史背景</summary>

**Yuan Mei, and the book he named after a quotation.** *Zibuyu* (《子不语》) takes its title from a single line in the *Analects*: **子不语怪、力、乱、神** — "the Master did not speak of the strange, the violent, the disorderly, and the divine" (Analects 7.21). Yuan Mei (袁枚, 1716–1798) then wrote a thousand-page book consisting entirely of the strange, the violent, the disorderly and the divine, and put the quotation on the cover as an announcement of what he was about to do. The joke was not lost on his contemporaries. In his own preface he defends the project with the argument that the sage's silence was a matter of *emphasis*, not prohibition — that the *Classic of Changes* speaks of the obscure, the *Book of Odes* records prodigies, and Zuo Qiuming, who studied directly under Confucius, filled his chronicles with exactly these four categories. His stated purpose is modest and slightly self-mocking: *"to drive out the commonplace with the absurd, and to rouse the indolent with the startling — is this not better than a game of chess?"* He also records that he first called the book *Zibuyu* and renamed it **Xīn Qíxié** (新齐谐, "New Records of the Strange") after discovering a Yuan-dynasty work with the same title, borrowing the second name from Zhuangzi's *Qíxié*, "the one who records the strange." Both titles stayed in circulation; the book is catalogued under both.

**The shape of the collection.** The standard Qing text — the **Suiyuan** (随园) edition printed under Yuan Mei's own supervision in 1788, the fifty-third year of the Qianlong reign — runs to **thirty-four volumes**: twenty-four volumes of *Zibuyu* proper containing **745 pieces**, and ten volumes of *Xu Zibuyu* (续子不语) containing **277**. That is 1,022 separate anecdotes, most of them shorter than this one, some of them a single paragraph, and a handful — 《李通判》, 《南昌士人》, 《鬼有三技》, and this one — that have been anthologised continuously for two centuries. Lu Xun, in the *Brief History of Chinese Fiction*, gave the book a verdict that has stuck: *"Its prose strips away ornament and comes close to the natural; but it is excessively casual, and much of it is rubbish — the author's own label, 'compiled for amusement' (戏编), is accurate."* Lu Xun meant it as criticism. It is also the best description of why the book still works: Yuan Mei wrote fast, wrote down whatever came to him, and never tried to make a tale more dignified than it was.

**Yuan Mei's qualifications.** He was born in Qiantang (Hangzhou) in 1716, passed the metropolitan examination in 1739 — the fourth year of Qianlong — and was appointed magistrate of **Lishui**, then of **Jiangning** (Nanjing). He was, by all accounts, a competent and honest official, and he hated the job. At forty he resigned, bought an abandoned garden in the hills below the Xiaocang Mountain in Nanjing — the former **Suiyuan** of the Jiangning textile commissioner — rebuilt it, renamed it with a homophone, and spent the next forty years there as a host, a cook, a garden designer, a teacher of poetry, and the most famous literary figure in the lower Yangtze. He took female students, which was scandalous. He wrote a cookbook, which was worse. And he wrote down every strange story that anybody told him, which was the whole point of the exercise. The *Zibuyu* anecdotes are full of the phrase **某公云** or **某某言** — "a certain gentleman said," "so-and-so told me" — because they are, structurally, gossip: a chain of transmission that Yuan Mei is the last and most gifted link in.

**Nanfeng and the end of the Ming.** The story's setting is specific: **Nanfeng County** (南丰县) in **Jianchang Prefecture** (建昌府), in the hill country of eastern Jiangxi, near the Fujian border. This was one of the areas hardest hit by the collapse of 1644–1650. When Beijing fell, the Ming state's fiscal apparatus in Jiangxi simply stopped functioning; when the Qing armies arrived, they arrived as an occupying force with a tax demand. Between those two facts lies a decade in which the only authorities that existed in a Jiangxi village were the ones that could be enforced locally: the lineage, the temple association, and the man with the tenants. The story's *háo shì* — the "strongman of the district" — is the product of exactly that gap. So is the bandit who has converted the village *shè* into a levy. Both are filling a hole where the county magistrate used to be. The difference between them, in Yuan Mei's telling, is that one of them went to the magistrate when the magistrate came back.

**Why a "society" was a racket.** The *shè* (社) is one of the oldest institutions in China — originally the altar of the local earth god, then the smallest unit of local administration, then, by the Ming, the ordinary name for the rotating village association that financed the great festivals of the rural year. These associations were genuinely communal: thirty households, a rotating treasurer, a pig, a hired troupe, a god carried out of his temple on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month and carried back in the evening. They were also, unavoidably, a **collection plate**. And any collection plate in a village with no audit and no fixed assessment is an instrument that can be played. Yuan Mei's precise phrase — 纠钱作社, "collecting money to run a *shè*" — describes the mechanism of the extortion rather than the extortion itself, which is how a good storyteller does it: he hands you the machine and lets you work out what it was being used for.

**The Thunder God's body.** The figure who descends into the flower garden is not an abstraction; he is a specific icon, and Yuan Mei's readers could have described him from the temple wall. The Ming-Qing standard, recorded in the Qing compendium *Jíshuō Quánzhēn*, is a squat, muscular, bare-chested male with **two wings inserted in his back**, a **third eye** in the forehead, a **face as red as a monkey's**, a **long pointed jaw**, feet like a hawk's, and claws sharper still; a **wedge** in the left hand, a **mallet** in the right, in the attitude of striking; and **five drums strung around his shoulders**. The pointed jaw gave the language the phrase **雷公嘴**, "Thunder Lord's mouth," which is still the ordinary Chinese way of describing a jutting, beaked chin — and which Wu Cheng'en used, in the sixteenth century, as one of the standard ways of describing Sun Wukong. The **thunder wedge** (*léixī*) is the most interesting item on the list: polished stone axe-heads, turned up by the plough in every province of China, which the countryside believed to be the chisels the thunder god used to split the roofs of the guilty. Qing antiquarians had already worked out that these were Neolithic tools. The belief survived them anyway, and survives in the phrase.

**The department.** Daoism turned this figure into an office — a Heavenly Worthy commanding thirty-six thunder lords, with a formal jurisdiction over "heaven's calamities and blessings" and "the living and the killing." The apparatus was assembled in the Song, when the Shenxiao school's thunder magic became one of the dominant liturgical technologies of the Daoist church, and by the Qing it was not esoteric doctrine but the standard furniture of popular religion.[^leibu] What matters for this story is that the office had the **shape of a government**, because it was modelled on one: departments, petitions, dockets, orders of execution. Yuan Mei's villagers are not praying. They are filing.

**Filth as a solvent of power.** The chamber pot is not a random object. It is, in Chinese popular religion, one of the few reliable weapons in existence — a principle written into the Han statutes on ritual purity, elaborated into the Blood Lake liturgies of the Southern Song, generalised by the Qing into a rule that any household filth dissolves spiritual power, and still operational in 1841, when the Qing commander Yang Fang had Guangzhou's chamber pots floated out on rafts toward the British steamers on the theory that the foreigners' invulnerability must be sorcery. The rafts capsized. Yuan Mei's joke was not, for his readers, an absurdity; it was a technique executed with unusual accuracy.[^huiwu]

**The argument in the garden.** What the grandfather says — *"in fifty years I have never seen you strike a tiger, but again and again I have seen you strike an ox"* — is an argument from observation, not doctrine, and it had been made formally seventeen centuries earlier by a man whose work Yuan Mei certainly knew. Wang Chong, in the *Lun Heng*, had already argued that thunder is fire, that it burns whatever is in the way, and that the iconography of the Thunder Lord is a human invention projected onto a physical event. Yuan Mei hands the identical argument to a Jiangxi farmer holding a chamber pot, and then lets the god concede it by falling into a field — the most characteristic move in the whole of *Zibuyu*.[^wangchong]

**The safe channel.** Direct criticism of the Qing magistracy was not survivable. The literary inquisitions of the eighteenth century — the **Zeng Jing** case of the 1730s and the **Xu Shukui** case of the 1770s, both within Yuan Mei's lifetime, both ending in executions and the destruction of entire private libraries — meant that a writer who described a county magistrate's court as a machine for selling verdicts to the highest petitioner could expect a visit from the provincial censor. The defence Yuan Mei uses here is the one Chinese writers had used since the *Strange Tales of Liaozhai*: **scale**. Move the court to heaven, make the magistrate a god, make the bribe a dish of pig's trotters, and the satire becomes a fairy tale — one that any reader walking home past the yamen gate could translate back into local terms in about four seconds. Yuan Mei never says that a Qing magistrate took money from the man who could pay. He only depicts a heaven that does. Lu Xun, who disliked the book's carelessness, still called its prose *"close to the natural"* — and this is what he meant. The story does not moralise. It shows you the machine, and then it shows you the machine falling into a rice paddy.

**Connection to other Cathay Tales.** This is the **tenth** tale on Cathay Tales drawn from Yuan Mei's collection (counting the sequel volume): [The Friend Who Forgot How to Leave](../posts/the-friend-who-forgot-how-to-leave) (Tale 005, 南昌士人), [The Magistrate Who Climbed Down Into Hell](../posts/the-magistrate-who-climbed-down-into-hell) (Tale 014, 酆都知县), [The Three Tricks Every Ghost Has](../posts/the-three-tricks-every-ghost-has) (Tale 022, 鬼有三技), [The Old Man Who Slept in His Own Coffin](../posts/the-old-man-who-slept-in-his-own-coffin) (Tale 031, 棺床), [The Skull That Blew Cold Air Through the Mat](../posts/the-skull-that-blew-cold-air-through-the-mat) (Tale 039, 骷髅吹气), [The Young Monk Who Could Not Forget the Tiger](../posts/the-young-monk-who-could-not-forget-the-tiger) (Tale 048, from 续子不语), [The Fox Scholar Who Passed the Immortal Exam](../posts/the-fox-scholar-who-passed-the-immortal-exam) (Tale 054, 狐生员劝人修仙), [The Sheep Trader Who Slept Above a Green-Haired Corpse](../posts/the-sheep-trader-who-slept-above-a-green-haired-corpse) (Tale 065, 绿毛怪), [The Cat That Grew Fangs in the Moonlight](../posts/the-cat-that-grew-fangs-in-the-moonlight) (Tale 073, 武林猫怪), and [The Old Servant Who Traded His Life](../posts/the-old-servant-who-traded-his-life) (Tale 082, 李通判). That last one is the closest cousin to this story: in 李通判 a fake Daoist priest who has been raising the dead for money is killed at the climax by a single **震雷一声** — one peal of thunder — and the corpse is found with seventeen characters of heaven's verdict burned into it in sulphur. Same author, same volume, same god, same method of execution, and the opposite outcome: in 李通判 the thunderbolt is *correct*. Yuan Mei wrote both. He was not making a claim about the thunder god's competence in general; he was making a claim about the specific reliability of *paperwork*.

## Translator's Note

Yuan Mei's thunder god is the most bureaucratic deity in Chinese fiction, and the most human: he can be misled by a dish of trotters, he can be shamed into silence, and he can be knocked out of the sky by a chamber pot and left roaring in a field for three days. What makes the tale last is not the slapstick but the courtroom inside it — the recognition that a man with no money has no way to answer a petition filed against him, and that his last resort is to make the whole proceeding dirty enough that heaven would rather drop it. Two hundred and forty years later, that is still the joke, and it is still not entirely a joke.

— *Wang Cai*
