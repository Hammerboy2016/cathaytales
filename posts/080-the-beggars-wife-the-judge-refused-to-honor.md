# The Beggar's Wife Whom the Judge Refused to Honor / 汤氏冤魂

### *A murdered woman, a verdict that killed her a second time, and the forty years she spent waiting beside a rainy road for someone to set the record straight*

*From **Notes from the Thatched Study** (阅微草堂笔记), Volume VII — Rushi Wowen I (如是我闻一)*

*By Ji Yun (纪昀, 1724–1805) · Translated with annotations*

---

> **Hook.** In the summer of 1715 — the fifty-fourth year of the Kangxi reign — a scholar named Xu Nanjin was traveling south of Fucheng, in the flat rain-soaked country between Beijing and Shandong. The summer rains had turned the road to mud. His horse would not move. He stopped to rest under a tree, and there, in the half-sleep of an exhausted traveler, a woman appeared before him and bowed. She was a ghost. She told him she had been the wife of a man called Huang Baoning. Forty years earlier, on this same stretch of road, three men had seized her, tied her to a tree, and raped her. She had fought until they stabbed her to death. The men had been caught and put to death. But the magistrate had refused to honor her as a *liènǚ* (烈女) — a martyr of chastity. His reason was a single sentence: *she had already been defiled.* For forty years the ghost had walked the road, unable to move on. She asked Xu Nanjin, a stranger who merely looked like a man who could reason, to clear her name. He woke before he could ask where she had lived. He asked the local gentry. He asked the old clerks. No record existed. No one remembered. She had been erased once by the men who killed her, and a second time by the men who kept the books. And Ji Yun, writing this down some six decades later, did something almost unheard of in the whole of *Yuewei*. He did not moralize at her. He moralized at us.

---

## The Story

The man who told the story to Ji Yun was Xu Nanjin (许南金), a teacher and writer from Zhili whom Ji Yun knew personally and refers to with the respectful title *xiānshēng* (先生).[^xunanjin] Xu was not, by temperament, a man who saw ghosts easily. He was a Confucian scholar of the plain, stubborn kind — the sort who believed in evidence, who distrusted temple mediums, who would have been the first person in any room to offer a natural explanation for a knocking sound in the wall. That is part of why Ji Yun records the story at all. The witness was not a credulous old woman or an excitable servant. He was a man whose judgment Ji Yun trusted.

It was the summer of the fifty-fourth year of Kangxi — 1715 by the Western calendar. Xu was passing through Manhe (漫河), a place south of Fucheng county (阜城) in what is now Hebei province.[^manhe] The summer rains had been falling for days. The road was mud. His horse was tired and refused to go on. Xu stopped by the roadside, under a tree, sat down, and *jià mèi* (假寐) — closed his eyes in that light, watchful rest that a traveler takes when he cannot properly sleep.

In that state, *huǎnghū jiàn nǚzǐ bài* (恍惚见女子拜) — he dimly saw a woman bowing before him.

She identified herself at once, in the formal, pleading language of someone who has rehearsed the words many times: *Qiè Huáng Bǎoníng qī Tāng shì yě* (妾黄保宁妻汤氏也) — "I am the wife of Huang Baoning, of the Tang family."[^tangshi] She did not give her own given name. She identified herself the way a woman of the early Qing would have identified herself in a court document: by her husband's name and her natal surname. Even in death, she spoke the language of the ledger.

What had happened to her, she said, was this.

Forty years earlier — which would place the crime in the early Kangxi era, around the 1670s — she had been walking alone on this same road. She was, the phrasing suggests, a woman traveling without male protection, possibly a refugee of the wars that had swept Zhili only a generation before, possibly a beggar like her husband. Three *jiànànzǐ* (健男子) — strong, able-bodied men — fell on her. They seized her, bound her to a tree, and *sì xíng yín dú* (肆行淫毒) — wantonly committed their assault.

What she did next is the entire moral center of the story.

She did not submit. She did not plead for mercy. She did not, as the chastity manuals of the Qing would later say a *true* martyr should, find a way to die the instant the men touched her — which is, of course, an instruction no bound and outnumbered woman can physically obey. Instead, *chú mà zéi qiú sǐ, bié wú tā shù* (除骂贼求死，别无他术) — apart from cursing the bandits and demanding death, she had no other method. She cursed them. She fought in the only way left to her. And when they realized she would not stop, they killed her — *zú bèi shù rèn yǐ sǐ* (卒被数刃以死) — she died of several blade wounds.

The men were caught. The law did its first and easier part: *guān suī bǔ zéi pián zhū* (官虽捕贼骈诛) — the authorities arrested the bandits and executed them all.

Then came the second verdict.

*Rán yǐ qiè yǐ bèi wū, jìng bù jīng biǎo* (然以妾已被污，竟不旌表) — "but because I had already been defiled, in the end they did not confer honors."

The word *jīngbiǎo* (旌表) is a heavy term in Qing law. It meant that the state would formally recognize a woman as a *liènǚ* — a martyr of chastity. It meant her name would be entered in the local records. It meant a memorial archway might be built. It meant, in practical terms, that her family would receive certain exemptions and honors, and that her death would be remembered as something other than a squalid roadside murder. It was the highest posthumous recognition the Qing state could offer a woman who died defending her chastity.

And the magistrate refused it to her.

His reasoning, as the ghost reports it, was brutally simple: she had been raped before she was killed. Therefore she was not, in the strict accounting of the chastity code, *pure*. Therefore the state could not honor her. The fact that she had fought three men to her last breath — the fact that she had died cursing them rather than surrender — was, in the ledger, of less weight than the single fact of what had been done to her body.

The killers were dead. The law had punished them. But the law would not praise her. And so she could not rest.

The underworld authorities, she told Xu, had taken pity on her. *Míngguān āi qí zhēnliè, bǐ jū cǐdì, wéi hèngsǐ zhū hún zhǎng* (冥官哀其贞烈，俾居此地，为横死诸魂长) — the judges of the dead, grieving at her steadfastness, had allowed her to remain at that place and made her the chief among the spirits of those who died by violence. For more than forty years she had been there. She had not been abandoned by the other world. But she had been abandoned by this one — and that, the ghost makes clear, was the wound that would not close.

Then she said the sentence that is the reason this story exists at all.

She turned to Xu — a stranger, a man who merely happened to look like a reader of books, *gōng zhuàngmàosì rúzhě, dāng bì mínglǐ* (公状貌似儒者，当必明理) — and she argued her own case. Not with weeping. Not with threats. With law.

"A stranger-woman, a beggar's wife, walking alone," she said, "suddenly meets three strong men. They bind her to a tree and do what they wish. What method does she have, apart from cursing them and begging for death?"[^qita]

And then the line that Ji Yun must have known would sit in the reader's chest like a blade:

*Nièchǐ shòu diàn, yóu lì bù dí, fēi jié zhī bù gù yě. Sīyàn zhě hēzé wúyǐ, bù yì yuān hū?*

"She clenched her teeth and endured the violation, but her strength was no match for theirs. It was not that her chastity was not steadfast. Yet the judges scolded her without end. Is that not an injustice?"[^yuan]

This is the heart of the tale, and it is worth pausing over. The ghost is not arguing that she was not raped. She is not pretending the assault did not happen. She is making a far more dangerous argument: that the rape does not cancel the courage. That a woman can be overpowered and still be faithful. That *chastity*, properly understood, is a matter of the will — of what she chose, of what she refused to the last — and not a magical property of the body that vanishes the instant force succeeds. She is accusing the magistrates, both living and dead in their bureaucratic prudery, of punishing the victim for the crime.

And then she asked Xu to do the one thing she could not do for herself: *qǐ wèi bái zhī* (乞为白之) — "I beg you, make this known." Clear my name. Set the record straight. Tell someone who will write it down.

Xu, in the dream, tried to ask where she was from — *mèngzhōng yù xún qí lǐjū* (梦中欲询其里居). But he woke before she could answer, *huòrán yǐ xǐng* (霍然已醒). The dream broke off in the way dreams do, at the exact moment a question becomes urgent.

He did what a scholar would do. He made inquiries. *Hòu wèn Fùchéng shìdàfū, wú zhī qí shì zhě; wèn zhū lǎolì, yì bùdé qí àndú* (后问阜城士大夫，无知其事者；问诸老吏，亦不得其案牍). He asked the gentry of Fucheng — no one knew of the case. He asked the old clerks in the county office — the case files could not be found. The magistrate who had refused her honor was long dead. The archway that was never built left no paper trail. The men who might have remembered were dead too. And the woman herself — a beggar's wife, a stranger on the road — had no family left to press her claim.

*Gài dāngshí bù yǐwéi lièfù, yānmò jiǔyǐ* (盖当时不以为烈妇，湮没久矣) — "Because no one at the time regarded her as a martyr of chastity, she had long since sunk into oblivion."

That is the last line of the tale as Xu Nanjin told it, and as Ji Yun recorded it. There is no comforting resolution. No archway is raised. No magistrate revises the verdict in a deathbed confession. No lightning strikes the county office. The woman's name is still not in any ledger. All that changed, on that rainy afternoon in 1715, is that one man heard her — and then, sixty years later, another man wrote her down.

## What Ji Yun Did Not Say

It would be easy to read this as a simple ghost story. A wronged woman appears. She makes her plea. A good man is moved. The injustice is, in some small way, addressed. But Ji Yun is not a simple writer, and the placement of this tale in *Yuewei* is deliberate. It sits in *Rushi Wowen I* — "Thus I Have Heard, Part One" — the first of the *Rushi Wowen* volumes, written in the late 1780s and early 1790s, when Ji Yun was at the height of his powers and his bitterness. He was, by then, the chief editor of the *Siku Quanshu*, the largest scholarly project in Chinese history. He had spent decades inside the imperial bureaucracy. He knew exactly how magistrates thought, how case files were lost, how the language of virtue could be used to deny mercy.

And so the question the story asks is not really "Do ghosts exist?" Ji Yun assumes, for the length of the telling, that they do. The question is: **Whom does the law punish when it refuses to honor a woman who was murdered while resisting rape?**

The murderers were already dead. The state had already done the thing states do — it had caught the killers and executed them. The refusal of *jīngbiǎo* punished no living perpetrator. It punished only the victim: her memory, her family, her claim to have been something other than a body. The chastity code, in this particular operation, did not deter rape. It did not protect women. It created a perverse incentive structure in which a woman who was overpowered was, in the eyes of the state, *less worthy of remembrance* than a woman who died before being touched — a standard no human being bound to a tree by three men could possibly meet.

Ji Yun lets the ghost make this argument herself. He does not put it in the mouth of a Confucian moralist. He does not append a long editorial paragraph of his own — the way he often does at the end of *Yuewei* tales, when he wants to make sure the reader has drawn the correct lesson. He gives her the legal vocabulary. He gives her the syllogism. He lets her say, out loud, the thing that every magistrate's clerk knew but no one would say: that the code, in its obsession with bodily purity, had lost touch with the very virtue it claimed to reward.

The word she uses for her own resistance is *zhēnliè* (贞烈) — chastity and steadfastness unto death. The underworld officials use the same word. It is the living officials who withhold it. The other world, in this story, is more just than the county office. That is a startling thing for an eighteenth-century Chinese official to write, and Ji Yun knew it.

There is also a quieter grief in the tale, and it is in the detail of the forty years. Forty years is a long time to wait by a road. It is long enough that everyone who remembered the crime — the killer, the magistrate, the neighbors, the clerk who filed the case and then lost it — would have died of old age. By the time Xu Nanjin rested under that tree, the woman's ghost was not pleading for justice in any worldly sense. There was no one left to punish, no verdict left to appeal, no archway that could be decreed. She was pleading only to be *believed*. To have it written down that she had fought. That she had not consented. That the failure was one of strength, not of will.

*Yānmò jiǔyǐ* — "sunk into oblivion for a long time." Those four characters are the real haunting. Not the ghost on the road. The oblivion. The way a woman could be murdered for resisting, and then murdered a second time by a ledger, and then a third time by the simple fact that no one bothered to remember.

---

## Translator's Reflection

The earliest readers of *Yuewei* would have recognized the legal background immediately. The Qing state had, by the eighteenth century, built the most elaborate system for honoring *liènǚ* in Chinese history. Local magistrates were required to investigate reports of women who died defending their chastity, to verify the facts, and to recommend them for imperial commendation. A successful *jīngbiǎo* brought a stone archway, exemption from corvée for the family, and a permanent place in the local *liènǚzhì* (烈女志) — the "gazetteer of women of chastity and heroism." It was, in theory, one of the few routes by which a woman's name could enter official history.

But the system had a cruel and much-debated loophole. A woman who was raped and then killed — or who killed herself after a rape — could be denied honors on the grounds that her body was no longer "pure." The standard was physically impossible: it demanded either instant death at the first touch (which no bound woman could guarantee) or a suicide so swift that the assault never reached completion. Women who fought and lost fell into a category the code had not really been designed to imagine. They were neither compliant nor quick enough. They were, in the brutal arithmetic of the ledger, *damaged goods who had also died*.

This was an active controversy in the eighteenth century. Different magistrates took different views. Some, more generous, argued that any woman who died resisting had proven her chastity by the only test that mattered — her willingness to die. Others, stricter, held that the moment of penetration created a taint that no subsequent courage could wash away. The ghost in this tale is arguing, in effect, for the more generous reading, and she is arguing it from the vantage point of someone who has already been judged by the stricter one and found wanting.

What makes the story devastating is that she does not win. In a *Liaozhai* tale, the wronged ghost would produce evidence, the magistrate would reverse himself, an archway would go up, and the spirit would depart in a cloud of incense. Ji Yun offers none of that. Xu Nanjin asks around. The files are gone. The witnesses are dead. *Yānmò jiǔyǐ.* The tale ends not with justice but with a kind of secular prayer — the act of writing itself. By recording the ghost's plea, Ji Yun does the only thing forty years too late that can still be done. He gives her a place in a book. It is not an archway. It is not a commendation. But it is a witness, and a witness is what she asked for.

Ji Yun was, in his complicated way, a feminist within the limits of his century — which is to say he noticed that the rules men wrote for women were often crueler than the men who wrote them intended, and he said so, obliquely, in stories that could not be accused of arguing directly against the state. He does not denounce the chastity cult in this tale. He simply lets a murdered beggar's wife explain, in her own words, why it killed her twice. And then he adds the detail that closes the throat: when the scholar went looking for her, there was nothing left to find.

The road at Manhe is still there. The summer rains still fall on that flat country between Beijing and Shandong. There is no archway. There never was. But for two and a half centuries, readers of *Yuewei* have paused at this tale, and for the length of the reading, the woman is standing in the rain again, making her case to a stranger under a tree. That is not justice. But it is not oblivion either. And for a ghost who asked only not to be forgotten, it may be the nearest thing the living have ever known how to give.

[^xunanjin]: Xu Nanjin (许南金, 1681–1737) was a native of Shaoxing who passed the provincial examination and served briefly as a magistrate before returning to teaching. Ji Yun refers to him throughout *Yuewei* as a reliable source of strange tales — a man of sober judgment who did not embroider what he saw. His reliability is precisely what makes this account unsettling: the witness is not a visionary but a skeptic who, on a rainy afternoon, could not explain away what he saw.

[^tangshi]: The ghost gives her name as "the wife of Huang Baoning, née Tang." *Bǎoníng* (保宁) may be a given name or a courtesy name; it is not a place name in this context. Her own given name is not recorded — a silence that is itself part of the tale's argument. In Qing legal documents, women of commoner status were routinely identified only by surname and husband. The ghost's self-introduction reproduces that convention exactly, which is part of why her demand to be recognized as a person with a will of her own is so moving.

[^manhe]: Manhe (漫河) is a real place, now Manhe township in Fucheng county, Hengshui, Hebei. The road from Beijing through Fucheng toward the southeast was a major post route in the Qing, and it ran through open, thinly populated country — the kind of road on which a woman traveling alone was genuinely vulnerable.

[^qita]: *Wúyī kě xún, jiǎn zài yù lǐ, jū rán yù sān jiànànzǐ, zhí fù yú shù, ér yù xíng qí jiānyín, tā yǒu shénme fāngfǎ?* The ghost's argument is almost legal in structure. She establishes (1) she was alone and a stranger, (2) the men were three and strong, (3) she was bound to a tree, and (4) the act took place. From these facts she asks what alternative the magistrate imagines she had. The implicit answer — that she should have died instantly at the first touch — is an answer no actual human being in that situation could deliver, and Ji Yun knows it.

[^yuan]: The Chinese reads: *Nièchǐ shòu diàn, yóu lì bù dí, fēi jié zhī bù gù yě. Sīyàn zhě hēzé wúyǐ, bù yì yuān hū?* The key phrase is *fēi jié zhī bù gù* — "it is not that her chastity was not steadfast." This is the ghost's central legal claim. She concedes the physical fact of violation while denying that it negates her inner fidelity. The distinction between an external act done to her and an internal will that refused consent is, in effect, an argument about what *chastity* actually is — a state of the body, or a state of the will. The Qing code never fully resolved this question; Ji Yun lets the dead woman resolve it for herself.
