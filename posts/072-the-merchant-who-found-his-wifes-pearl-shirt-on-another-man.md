# The Merchant Who Found His Wife's Pearl Shirt on Another Man / 蒋兴哥重会珍珠衫

### *Volume 1 of Feng Menglong's Stories to Illustrate the World — the pearl shirt that traveled from a wife's bedroom to a stranger's back and back again*

*From **Stories to Illustrate the World** (喻世明言, c. 1620), Volume 1 — collected by Feng Menglong (冯梦龙, 1574–1646) · Translated and annotated by Cathay Tales*

---

> A young merchant in Xiangyang married a woman so beautiful he could not bear to leave her for a business trip. He went anyway. She gave him a pearl shirt to remember her by. Eleven months later, in a teahouse in another province, a stranger from Huizhou bragged about an affair with a beauty in Xiangyang — and showed the pearl shirt as proof. The stranger did not know he was talking to the husband. What the husband did next — and what happened after that — is the story.

---

## I. A Merchant's Son, a Beauty, and a Marriage That Was Too Good

In the Jiajing reign of the Ming dynasty, in the city of **Xiangyang** (襄阳) in Huguang province, there lived a man named **Jiang De** (蒋德), whose courtesy name was **Xingge** (兴哥). His father, **Jiang Shize** (蒋世泽), was a successful merchant who had spent thirty years buying and selling between Xiangyang and the southern ports — first Canton (广州), then the markets along the Pearl River — and had built the family into one of the comfortable middle-rank households inside the city walls.

Jiang Shize's wife had died young. He raised his son alone and, when Xingge came of age, married him to the daughter of a Wang family in the neighboring county. The bride's childhood name was **Sanqiao** (三巧儿) — *Third Delicate* — and she had grown up into a woman of the kind of beauty that stops traffic in the street and makes strangers turn their heads at teahouse windows. Her face was like the full moon in the eighth month. Her skin was white jade. Her lips were cherries. Her small bound feet — barely three inches — stepped so lightly that the neighbors said she could walk across a rice-paper screen without tearing it.

The marriage, in a city where most marriages were merely arrangements of property and family connection, was genuinely happy.

Jiang Xingge was twenty-four. Wang Sanqiao was sixteen. He had the easy manner of a merchant's son who had been raised around money but had not been spoiled by it. She had the quiet sweetness of a girl who had been raised by decent parents in a county town and had never seen cruelty up close. The two of them fit together in the way that the matchmaker's rhymes promise but rarely deliver. They were, in the old phrase, *the match that heaven arranged* (天作之合).

Two years passed. They had no children yet, but the household was content. Jiang Xingge had taken over the running of the family's trading business from his father, who had retired to a quiet life in the back rooms of the house, tending a small garden and drinking tea.

Then Jiang Shize fell ill.

The illness was not dramatic. A cough that would not go away. A weariness that no amount of ginseng tea could lift. The local physician came twice, prescribed herbal decoctions, shook his head on the way out. By the seventh week Jiang Shize was bedridden. By the ninth week he called his son to his side and said: *I am going to die. There is a debt of three thousand strings of cash owed to our family by a merchant house in Canton. Your grandfather lent it to them forty years ago and they have never paid. Go to Canton. Collect the debt. And do not come home until you have it.*

Jiang Xingge said: *Father, I cannot leave you.*

Jiang Shize said: *You can leave me. I am dying anyway. The debt is not dying — it is forty years old and growing older. Go.*

Jiang Shize died three days later. The funeral was conducted according to the proper rites — seven weeks of mourning, the burning of paper houses and paper servants, the hiring of monks to chant sutras for the dead man's passage through the ten courts of hell. Wang Sanqiao performed the widow's role with quiet grace: she wore the plain white mourning hemp, she knelt at the altar, she wept at the appropriate moments.

When the forty-nine days were over, Jiang Xingge sat with his wife in the inner room and told her what his father had said.

*Three thousand strings of cash,* he said. *In Canton.*

Wang Sanqiao said: *How long will you be gone?*

*A year. Perhaps eleven months. The southern roads are fast in the dry season — I will go in the ninth month and be home before the new year.*

She did not argue. She did not weep — not then. She went to the wooden chest at the foot of their bed and brought out a garment: a **pearl shirt** (珍珠衫), a close-fitting undershirt of fine white silk with over a hundred tiny freshwater pearls sewn into the collar and cuffs in a pattern of plum blossoms. It was her dowry piece — the single most valuable item she had brought into the marriage, worth perhaps fifty strings of cash on its own.

She held it out to him.

*Wear this on the road,* she said. *When you look down at your chest and see the pearls, you will remember me. And when you come home, I will still be here.*

Jiang Xingge took the pearl shirt. He held it for a long time. Then he packed it carefully in the innermost fold of his traveling bundle, kissed his wife's forehead, and left for Canton on a morning in the ninth month.

---

## II. The Eleven Months

The southern road from Xiangyang to Canton was a fourteen-hundred-li journey through the mountainous interior of Huguang and into the subtropical lowlands of Guangdong. A fast rider could do it in twenty days. A merchant on foot with goods to transport — even a merchant traveling light, as Jiang Xingge was, carrying only sample goods and the family's debt documents — needed thirty to forty days.

He reached Canton in the tenth month, found the merchant house that owed the debt, and began the long process of collecting. The debt was real — the documents were in order, the seals matched, the original contract bore the chop of the debtor's grandfather — but the current head of the merchant house, a man named **Liu** (刘), claimed the debt had been settled decades ago through a verbal agreement that his father had never bothered to write down. The argument took weeks. Then months.

Jiang Xingge settled in a small inn near the **Thirteen Factories** (十三行) district on the Pearl River, where the foreign traders — Portuguese, occasionally Dutch — kept their warehouses. He wrote home once a month. His letters, in the early months, were warm and detailed: he described the lychee trees along the river, the strange southern fruits he had never seen, the heat that came up off the water at night like a wet blanket. He asked about his mother-in-law, about the household, about the small garden his wife tended in the back courtyard.

Wang Sanqiao's replies, carried north by the monthly merchant-post, were shorter but equally warm. She reported on the household. She asked about his health. She reminded him to eat properly and not to drink too much southern rice wine. In the fifth letter — the one that reached him in the second month — she wrote: *The plum tree in the back courtyard has not bloomed yet. I am saving the first branch for your return.*

By the sixth month of his stay, the letters had become shorter on both sides. Not cold — just shorter. The kind of short that comes from repetition: *I am well. Are you well? When will you come home?* The words were the same. The space between them had changed.

The debt was not settled until the eleventh month.

Liu's merchant house finally agreed to pay — not the full three thousand strings, but twenty-two hundred, which Jiang Xingge accepted after two days of negotiation. The money was converted into silver ingots and packed into a reinforced leather bag. The documents were stamped and sealed. The business was done.

On the morning after the final payment, Jiang Xingge settled his inn bill, packed his bags, and set out for the north road.

He had been in Canton for eleven months. He had been away from Xiangyang for nearly thirteen — counting the travel time each way.

## III. What Happened at Home While He Was Gone

The story now leaves Jiang Xingge on the north road and turns back to Xiangyang — to the house inside the city walls where Wang Sanqiao had been living alone for nearly a year.

She had not been living *entirely* alone. The old father-in-law had died, but the household still included two servant women — **Qingmei** (晴梅), who had been with the family for eight years, and a younger girl named **Cuiyun** (翠云) — and the next-door neighbor, a woman everyone called **Aunt He** (何妈), a widow who sold silk thread from her front room and whose back wall was shared with the Jiangs' back courtyard.

Wang Sanqiao had spent the first three months of her husband's absence quietly: reading, embroidering, tending the garden, visiting her mother in the neighboring county twice. By the fourth month, the quiet had begun to feel heavier. By the sixth month, she had started spending more time at the second-floor window that faced the street.

The street, in that part of Xiangyang, was a minor commercial road — not the main market but busy enough for a steady flow of sedan chairs, merchant carts, monk processions, and wandering peddlers. Wang Sanqiao would sit at the window in the afternoons, embroidering, and watch the street below. It was the only entertainment she had.

One afternoon in the seventh month — the hottest part of the summer, when the air above the flagstones shimmered — a young man walked past the house. He was not from Xiangyang. He was from **Huizhou** (徽州) in southern Anhui province, a merchant who had come to Huguang to buy grain. His name was **Chen Shang** (陈商), and everyone called him **Chen Dalang** (陈大郎) — *Big Chen* — not because he was physically large but because he was the eldest son of a Huizhou merchant family and carried himself with the brisk confidence of a man who had never been told no.

Chen Shang was twenty-six, unmarried, and possessed of what the storyteller's formula calls *a face like polished jade and a manner like spring wind* — that is, he was extremely handsome and extremely charming. He was also, as the formula does not mention, extremely bored. He had been in Xiangyang for three weeks on grain business, the deals were done, he was waiting for his caravan to assemble for the return trip, and he had nothing to do.

He looked up at the second-floor window.

Wang Sanqiao was there.

The storyteller says that when Chen Shang saw her, *his soul left his body and his bones went soft* (魂不附体，骨软筋酥). He stood in the middle of the street, looking up, until a sedan chair nearly ran him over.

He spent the rest of the afternoon in a teahouse, thinking about the woman at the window. He spent the next three days trying to think about anything else. On the fourth day, he went back to the street and stood under the window again.

Wang Sanqiao was there. She looked down. Their eyes met.

She looked away.

But she did not close the window.

---

## IV. Aunt Xue

The Huizhou merchant — like all merchants in any Chinese city who find themselves stranded without entertainment — knew where to find a go-between. In Xiangyang, the woman for the job was **Xue Po** (薛婆) — *Aunt Xue* — a professional matchmaker, part-time fortune-teller, and full-time facilitator of arrangements that married people did not want their spouses to know about.

Aunt Xue was in her late fifties, a compact, sharp-eyed woman with a mouth that could smile and calculate at the same time. She had been in the business for thirty years. She knew every household inside the city walls — who was happy, who was bored, who was vulnerable, and what price each category cost. She also sold cosmetics — face powder, hair oil, embroidered hairpins — which gave her a legitimate reason to call on any married woman in the city.

Chen Shang found her in a teahouse on the fifth day after he had first seen Wang Sanqiao. He described the woman at the window — the Jiang merchant's wife, the one on the side street near the Drum Tower. Aunt Xue nodded before he finished.

*Jiang Xingge's wife,* she said. *Wang Sanqiao. Yes. I know the household.*

*Can you arrange it?*

Aunt Xue did not answer immediately. She poured a cup of tea, sipped it, and set the cup down.

*The woman is decent,* she said. *Not the kind who falls for strangers at windows. But she has been alone for months. Her husband is in Canton. The household is quiet. These are conditions that can be — managed.*

*What is the price?*

Aunt Xue named a figure. It was high. Chen Shang paid it without bargaining.

Over the next two weeks, Aunt Xue executed a campaign of infiltration that the storyteller describes with the precision of a military dispatch. She began with the servant girls — buying them small gifts, sitting with them in the kitchen, learning the household's daily routine. She then called on the next-door neighbor, Aunt He, with a gift of face powder and a story about needing a quiet place to do her accounts. Aunt He, who was both lonely and susceptible to free cosmetics, invited her in.

By the end of the first week, Aunt Xue had established herself as a frequent visitor to the Jiang household. She brought cosmetics for Wang Sanqiao — samples of a new Guangzhou face powder, a set of embroidered hairpins from Suzhou — and sat in the front room chatting while Wang Sanqiao worked on her embroidery. She talked about the world outside: the new silk fashions in Suzhou, the price of rice in Hunan, the young men she had seen in the street. She mentioned, casually, that there was a young Huizhou merchant in town — very handsome, very polite, from a good family — who was looking for a local connection for future trade.

Wang Sanqiao listened. She did not ask questions. She did not express interest. She finished her embroidery and went inside.

Aunt Xue came back the next week. And the next.

On the fourth visit, she brought a small package: a bolt of fine Huizhou silk, the kind that Xiangyang did not sell. She said she had bought it at a good price from the Huizhou merchant's stock and had extra — would Madam Jiang like a length for a summer gown?

Wang Sanqiao hesitated. The silk was beautiful. The price was low. Accepting a small gift from a neighbor's go-between was not improper — it was normal.

She accepted.

Aunt Xue came back the next week with another gift — this time a pair of gold hairpins. Wang Sanqiao accepted these too, but more reluctantly. Aunt Xue said: *The young merchant sends his regards. He saw you at the window and was struck by your beauty. He does not wish to cause trouble — he only hopes to be of service.*

Wang Sanqiao said: *I am a married woman.*

Aunt Xue said: *Of course you are. And your husband is in Canton, is he not? A young woman alone in a large house — what harm in a polite acquaintance?*

Wang Sanqiao did not say yes. She did not say no. She put the gold hairpins in her jewelry box.

Aunt Xue understood.

## V. The Affair

The arrangement was made on a rainy afternoon in the eighth month.

Aunt Xue came to the house in the morning, when Qingmei was at the market and Cuiyun was washing clothes in the back courtyard. She told Wang Sanqiao that the rain had made the roads muddy and she did not want to walk home — could she rest in the front room for an hour?

Wang Sanqiao said yes.

Aunt Xue sat in the front room and drank tea. After half an hour, she said: *The young merchant is outside. He took shelter from the rain under your eaves. He would like to thank you in person for accepting his small gifts — just a cup of tea, nothing more.*

Wang Sanqiao said: *No.*

Aunt Xue said: *He is already here. If you do not see him, it will be awkward for both of us.*

Wang Sanqiao said nothing for a long time. Then she said: *Let him come in.*

Chen Shang came in. He was, as the storyteller promises, extremely handsome. He was wearing a clean blue scholar's robe — not the merchant's traveling clothes he had worn in the street — and his hair was freshly tied with a jade pin. He bowed deeply, with the particular kind of deference that makes a young woman feel both respected and desired.

*Madam Jiang,* he said. *I am ashamed to intrude. I am a stranger in your city and I have been — foolish. I saw you at the window and I have not been able to think of anything else for a month. I know I should not be here. I know your husband will be home soon. I only wanted to tell you — once — that you are the most beautiful woman I have ever seen.*

Wang Sanqiao stood in the doorway between the front room and the inner room. She was wearing a plain cotton gown. Her hair was tied back in a simple knot. She had not put on the gold hairpins.

She said: *You should leave.*

Chen Shang bowed again. *I will leave. But may I come back?*

Wang Sanqiao did not answer.

He came back three days later. And again three days after that. And again after that.

By the end of the eighth month, the affair was established. By the ninth month, it was regular. Chen Shang rented a small room at an inn on the next street. Aunt Xue served as lookout and messenger. The servant girls were paid off — not much, but enough. Wang Sanqiao sent them to the market on errands during the hours when Chen Shang visited.

The storyteller, who has been patient with Wang Sanqiao up to this point, now pauses to deliver a moral comment:

*The ancient saying goes: a woman's virtue is like a bottle of clear water — place it on a high shelf and no one will touch it; set it on the ground and every passing foot will kick it over. Wang Sanqiao was not a bad woman. She was a lonely woman in a city where her husband had left her for eleven months, and a go-between came with gifts and a handsome face and a story that flattered her. The bottle was set on the ground. The feet came.*

## VI. The Pearl Shirt

In the tenth month, Chen Shang's caravan was ready to return to Huizhou. He had been in Xiangyang for four months — far longer than any grain deal required. His servants were restless. His grain had long since been loaded and shipped.

On his last night in Xiangyang, he went to the Jiang house one final time.

Wang Sanqiao had packed a small bundle for him: two bolts of Xiangyang silk, a pair of embroidered shoes (his size — she had measured them secretly), and the **pearl shirt**.

She took it off her own body — she had been wearing it under her gown, against her skin, since the ninth month, when Chen Shang had first asked her for a keepsake. The pearls were warm from her body heat. The silk was soft.

*Take this,* she said. *When you are far away, wear it next to your skin and remember me.*

Chen Shang took the pearl shirt. He put it on immediately, under his traveling clothes. He said: *I will wear it every day.*

Wang Sanqiao said: *Do not let anyone see it. If anyone asks where you got it, say you bought it in Xiangyang at a market.*

Chen Shang promised. He left the next morning.

Wang Sanqiao returned to her quiet household. The servant girls went back to their routines. Aunt Xue stopped visiting — there was no further business to conduct. The back courtyard was still. The plum tree had not bloomed.

Three months passed.

---

## VII. The Teahouse in a Different Province

Jiang Xingge was on the north road, three days out of Canton, when he stopped at a large inn in a town called **Chenzhou** (郴州) in southern Huguang — a waystation on the main merchant road between Guangdong and the central provinces. The inn was full of northbound merchants, all heading home after the autumn trading season. The dining room was noisy, the tea was bad, and the beds were hard. Jiang Xingge did not mind. He was two weeks from home.

At the next table sat a young man in a Huizhou merchant's traveling clothes — blue robe, jade hairpin, a small traveling chest strung with a leather cord. He was drinking alone, talking loudly to no one in particular, and displaying, with the particular confidence of a man who has successfully seduced a married woman and wants the world to know it, a very unusual undershirt.

He had taken off his outer robe in the heat of the dining room and was sitting in his sleeveless undershirt — a garment of fine white silk with over a hundred tiny freshwater pearls sewn into the collar and cuffs in a pattern of plum blossoms.

Jiang Xingge looked at the pearl shirt.

He knew that shirt.

He had seen his wife sew those pearls — one by one — on winter evenings when the oil lamp was low. He had watched her thread the needle. He had watched her knot each pearl into the silk. He had worn that shirt himself for the first three months of his journey, until she asked him to put it away because the traveling was too rough and the pearls might come loose.

He looked at the young man from Huizhou.

The young man, noticing Jiang Xingge's stare, smiled broadly and said: *You like the shirt? It is a fine piece, is it not? I will tell you how I got it — and you will envy me.*

Jiang Xingge said nothing. He poured himself more tea.

The young man from Huizhou — Chen Shang — was drunk. He was also, as the storyteller says, *a man whose mouth was wider than his gate* — that is, he could not keep a secret to save his life. He had spent four months in Xiangyang carrying on an affair with a beautiful married woman, and he had been carrying the story around inside him like a pot of boiling water with no vent. Now, in a teahouse full of strangers a thousand li from Xiangyang, he opened the vent.

*This shirt,* he said, leaning across the table, *was given to me by the most beautiful woman in Xiangyang. A merchant's wife — her husband was in Canton for over a year. She lived alone. A go-between introduced us. She fell for me in a week.*

He pulled the collar of the shirt open to show the pearl pattern.

*She gave me this. Her own pearl shirt — over a hundred pearls, hand-sewn. Worth at least fifty strings of cash. She wore it next to her skin and gave it to me as a love token.*

He leaned closer.

*Her name,* he said, lowering his voice to a theatrical whisper, *is Wang Sanqiao. Third Delicate. And she is — how should I say it — she is not the kind of woman who gives her pearl shirt to just anyone.*

Jiang Xingge sat very still.

He looked at the shirt. He looked at the man. He looked at the shirt again.

Then he said, very quietly: *Your tea is cold. Let me buy you a fresh pot.*

Chen Shang, delighted to have found a willing listener, accepted. He spent the next hour telling Jiang Xingge everything — the go-between, the servant girls, the rainy afternoon, the window, the silk, the gold hairpins, the final night, the pearl shirt.

Jiang Xingge listened. He did not interrupt. He did not ask questions. He did not show anger.

When Chen Shang finally ran out of things to say, Jiang Xingge stood up, bowed politely, and said: *I have business in the next room. Excuse me.*

He walked to his room. He closed the door. He sat on the edge of the bed.

The storyteller does not describe what he felt. The storyteller says only: *His face went white. His hands trembled. He sat there for a very long time.*

## VIII. The Return

Jiang Xingge reached Xiangyang on a winter morning, eleven days before the new year.

He did not go straight home. He went to his father's old study — a small room at the back of the house that he used as an office — and sat down at the desk. He took out a sheet of paper. He took out a brush. He sat there for a long time.

Then he wrote.

He did not write a letter to his wife. He wrote a letter to his mother-in-law — **Madam Wang** (王婆), the woman who had raised Wang Sanqiao, who lived in the neighboring county. The letter was brief:

*Respected Mother Wang — I have returned from Canton. I regret to inform you that your daughter and I can no longer continue as husband and wife. The reason is known to heaven and to her. I have enclosed a divorce paper. Please arrange for her to return to your household. I will not pursue the matter further. — Jiang De.*

He sealed the letter. He gave it to a servant to carry to Madam Wang's house. Then he went to the inner rooms of his own house.

Wang Sanqiao was in the back courtyard, watering the plum tree. When she saw him, she dropped the water bucket and ran to him. She was crying. She said: *You are home. You are home. I have been waiting for you every day.*

Jiang Xingge looked at her. He did not embrace her. He did not speak.

He went inside and closed the door.

The next morning, Madam Wang arrived from the neighboring county. She came with the divorce paper. She was furious. She stood in the front room and shouted at her daughter: *You have shamed this family. You have shamed your dead father. You have shamed the Jiang family who treated you like their own.*

Wang Sanqiao knelt on the floor and wept. She did not deny it. She said: *I know. I know what I have done. I will go.*

She packed a single trunk — her clothes, her embroidery, her small personal items. She did not take the gold hairpins. She did not take the Huizhou silk. She left the pearl shirt's twin — the receipt for the pearls, a small silk tag sewn into the lining — on the bed.

She went back to her mother's house.

Jiang Xingge did not come to the door to watch her leave.

---

## IX. The Magistrate's Concubine

Two years passed.

Wang Sanqiao lived at her mother's house in the neighboring county. She did not go out. She did not see neighbors. She spent her days embroidering and her nights reading — she had always been literate, and her mother's small collection of storybooks kept her company. She grew thin. She stopped wearing cosmetics. The servants whispered that she was going to die of grief.

She did not die. But she changed.

In the second year, Madam Wang — who was herself in difficult financial circumstances, the divorce having cut off the Jiang family's financial support — arranged for her daughter to become the concubine of a newly appointed county magistrate named **Wu Jie** (吴杰). Magistrate Wu was a middle-aged man from Zhejiang province, a proper degree-holder who had passed the provincial examination in his thirties and the metropolitan examination two years later. He had been assigned to a small county in Huguang — not the prestigious posting he had hoped for, but respectable. He was a decent man. He had a wife in Zhejiang who had not accompanied him to the posting (she was managing the family estate), and he was permitted, under the Qing code, to keep a concubine at his yamen.

Wang Sanqiao became **Third Concubine Wang** (王三姨). She performed her duties with the same quiet competence she had brought to the Jiang household. Magistrate Wu was pleased with her. She was beautiful, literate, and — most important — she did not complain.

Life at the yamen was orderly. The magistrate conducted his cases. The clerks processed the paperwork. Third Concubine Wang managed the inner rooms.

One afternoon, Magistrate Wu came to her quarters with an unusual expression.

*I had a case today,* he said. *A divorce dispute. A merchant from Xiangyang petitioned to annul his former wife's remarriage on the grounds that she had committed adultery before the divorce was finalized.*

Third Concubine Wang said: *What happened?*

*I dismissed the petition. The divorce was properly executed — the divorce paper was signed, the wife returned to her parents' home, and she has now been legitimately remarried. The former husband has no grounds for annulment.*

She said: *What was the husband's name?*

Magistrate Wu looked at her. *His name is Jiang De. Courtesy name Xingge. From Xiangyang.*

Third Concubine Wang was very still.

The magistrate looked at her for a long time. Then he said, quietly: *You are his former wife.*

It was not a question.

Wang Sanqiao knelt. She did not weep. She said: *Yes. I was Wang Sanqiao, the wife of Jiang De of Xiangyang. I committed adultery while he was away. He divorced me. I am ashamed.*

Magistrate Wu sat down. He poured himself a cup of tea. He drank it slowly.

Then he said: *I am going to do something that no magistrate should do. I am going to invite your former husband to the yamen — not as a litigant, but as a guest. I want to meet him. And then I am going to ask you what you want.*

## X. The Reunion

Jiang Xingge came to the yamen the next day. He was wearing his best merchant's robe. He brought a gift of Canton tea for the magistrate. He did not know why he had been summoned.

Magistrate Wu received him in the study. They drank tea. They talked about the weather, the harvest, the price of grain. After half an hour, the magistrate said:

*Mr. Jiang, I know your story. Your former wife is now in my household. She is a good woman who made a serious mistake. I want to ask you one question, and I want you to answer honestly. Do you still love her?*

Jiang Xingge put down his teacup. He was silent for a long time.

Then he said: *I loved her very much. I could not bear to leave her for a business trip. I left anyway. She was alone. A man from Huizhou seduced her. A go-between facilitated it. She gave him a pearl shirt — a shirt she had sewn with her own hands, a shirt I had worn on the road. When I found out, I could not — I could not continue.*

He stopped. He looked at the magistrate.

*I divorced her. I sent her back to her mother. I have not seen her in two years. I have not remarried. I think about her every day.*

The magistrate said: *Then I will do something that no magistrate should do. I am going to return her to you.*

He sent for Wang Sanqiao.

She came in. She was wearing the plain grey gown of a magistrate's concubine. Her hair was simply tied. She was thinner than she had been. She looked at Jiang Xingge.

They looked at each other for a long time.

Then Magistrate Wu said: *I am going to give you a gift. Your former wife — the woman who wronged you, and who has paid for it — I am going to return her to you. Take her home. Treat her well. And Jiang De — do not go to Canton again for eleven months.*

Jiang Xingge knelt. Wang Sanqiao knelt. Both of them wept.

Magistrate Wu, who was — as the storyteller insists — a genuinely good man, gave Wang Sanqiao a set of traveling clothes and a small dowry of silver from his own funds to help the couple restart their household. He wrote a letter of introduction to the Xiangyang magistrate, asking that no further legal trouble be brought against the former adulteress.

Jiang Xingge and Wang Sanqiao left the yamen together. They walked through the county town in the late afternoon sun. They did not speak. They held hands.

## XI. Afterword — The Pearl Shirt

This story, in Feng Menglong's telling, is not primarily about adultery or forgiveness. It is about a garment.

The pearl shirt — the *pearl shirt* — is the physical object that makes the entire plot possible. It is a love token given by a wife to her traveling husband. It becomes a love token given by the same wife to her lover. It becomes the evidence that exposes the affair. It becomes the reason for the divorce. And in the final scene — which Feng Menglong describes in a single sentence, as if it were too important to elaborate — Wang Sanqiao brings the pearl shirt out of her traveling trunk when she and Jiang Xingge are back in their old house in Xiangyang, and she gives it to him again.

*She took the pearl shirt from the bottom of the trunk and placed it in his hands. The pearls were the same. The silk was the same. The plum-blossom pattern was the same. Only the person who had worn it had changed.*

The shirt is, in the storyteller's metaphor, a mirror. It reflects whoever wears it. When the husband wore it, it was faithfulness. When the wife wore it, it was desire. When the lover wore it, it was betrayal. When the husband saw it on the lover, it was truth. And when the wife gave it back to the husband at the end, it was — what? Forgiveness? Shame? Love?

Feng Menglong, who compiled this story from an older Song dynasty street-tale and rewrote it in the vernacular of the late Ming, does not say. He ends the story with a four-line poem:

> *The pearl shirt goes from hand to hand,*
> *but the heart that sewed it stays the same.*
> *Do not blame the woman at the window —*
> *blame the road that took her man away.*

It is, for a seventeenth-century Chinese storyteller, a remarkably sympathetic ending. The blame, in Feng Menglong's telling, falls not on the adulteress but on the structure that made the adultery possible: the merchant's road, the months of absence, the loneliness, the go-between, the handsome face, the window. The pearl shirt is not a symbol of sin. It is a symbol of distance — the physical distance between a husband and a wife that made everything else possible.

The story was first told as a street performance in the Song dynasty — a *huaben*, a prompt-text for a professional storyteller who would perform it in a teahouse for an audience of merchants, clerks, and soldiers. The audience would have recognized the plot immediately: it was, and is, the oldest story in the world. Husband leaves. Wife is alone. Stranger comes. Things happen. Husband returns. What now?

What Feng Menglong added to the old story was the ending: not punishment, not death, not monastic retirement, but reunion. The pearl shirt goes full circle. The marriage, broken and remade, is stronger at the end than it was at the beginning — because it has been tested, and because both people now know what it costs.

It is, perhaps, the most forgiving story in all of Chinese vernacular fiction.
