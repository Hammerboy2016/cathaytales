# The Ghost Hunter Who Caught Nothing / 鬼避姜三莽

### *He heard that a ghost could be tied up, spat on, turned into a sheep and sold at the butcher's market — so he bought a rope, and for a month the ghosts of Xian County stayed home*

*From **Notes from the Thatched Study** (阅微草堂笔记), Volume VIII — Rushi Wowen II (如是我闻二)*

*By Ji Yun (纪昀, 1724–1805) · Translated with annotations*

---

## The Story

### I. A Man Named Jiang Sanmang

The first thing Ji Yun tells us is where the man lived. **Jingcheng** (景城) is a village in **Xian County**, in the flat grey farmland of central Zhili[^jingcheng] — the province that is now Hebei — about a hundred and fifty kilometres south of Beijing, in country so level that a man standing on a roof can see the next county's smoke. It was Ji Yun's own ancestral home. His family had been settled there for generations, and when he writes "there was a man in Jingcheng named Jiang Sanmang," he is writing about a neighbour, or a neighbour's tenant, or somebody his father had pointed out across a field.

The second thing he tells us is what the man was like: **勇而憨** — bold, and thick. The word 憨 is not simply "stupid." It describes a person whose understanding has no side doors: honest, literal, slow to doubt, impossible to frighten because he has never quite grasped what there is to be frightened of. English has no single word for it. "Simple" is too kind, "foolish" too harsh, and "reckless" misses the innocence. He is a man who takes instructions literally and stories at face value.

The name itself is a village name. **Sanmang** — "Third Reckless," or "Mang the Third" — is not what a family would put in a genealogy. It is what the lane called him: the third son of the Jiang household, with 莽 attached, a word that means rank grass, and therefore a man who grows where he is not wanted and does not know it. In the registers he would be Jiang San; in the story he is Jiang Sanmang, and the nickname is doing most of the work of characterisation before a single thing has happened.

The third thing Ji Yun tells us is how the story reached him: **姚安公闻先曾祖润生公言** — "My father, Lord Yao'an, heard my great-grandfather Runsheng say it." Three generations and one death before the story was written down. This is the ordinary machinery of the *Notes from the Thatched Study*: Ji Yun spent his last years collecting what his relatives, his servants, his colleagues and his dinner guests told him, and he almost always tells you who told him, because the chain of transmission is part of the evidence. A story that has come through four mouths is not a fact; it is a thing people keep saying. For Ji Yun that was enough to be worth writing down, and for us it is the reason the book still reads like a village.

### II. The Book That Taught Him Ghosts Could Be Tied

One day Jiang Sanmang heard somebody tell the story of **Song Dingbo**.

It is one of the oldest ghost stories in Chinese, and by the eighteenth century it was as widely known as any tale in the language. A young man of Nanyang was walking a night road when he fell in with a stranger. The stranger asked who he was. Song Dingbo, deciding quickly, said he was a ghost too. The stranger believed him, and they walked on together, and because ghosts travel light and men travel heavy, they agreed to take turns carrying each other. Song Dingbo, on the ghost's back, was suspiciously weighty; the ghost said so. Song Dingbo said he had just come from a funeral and was still heavy with the dead. They came to a river. The ghost crossed in silence; Song Dingbo made a splash, and had to explain that he was newly dead and did not yet know how to cross water without noise. Then Song Dingbo put the ghost on his own shoulders and held on.

At the market town of Wancheng, he set it down on the ground. It became a sheep. He was afraid it would change back, so he spat on it — spit being, in the logic of the tale, a small dose of living human force that fixes a thing in its shape — and sold it. The price is the detail that mattered to Jiang Sanmang: **一千五百钱**, fifteen hundred cash.[^songdingbo]

Ji Yun does not retell any of this. He assumes you know it, the way a modern writer assumes you know the plot of *Cinderella*. He gives one line of summary — "he heard people tell of Song Dingbo selling a ghost for money" — and then moves straight to the reaction, which is the whole story:

> **大喜曰：「吾今乃知鬼可缚。」**
>
> *He was delighted, and said: "So now I know that a ghost can be tied up."*

That is the sentence the tale turns on. Not "ghosts exist" — he never doubted that. Not "I am brave" — he never thought about it. What reached him, out of a thousand-year-old story, was a **procedural** detail: the thing can be bound. Which means it has a body. Which means it has a market.

### III. The Business Plan

What follows is one of the great comic monologues in Qing literature, and it is comic because it is so exactly a business plan:

> **「如每夜缚一鬼，唾使变羊，晓而牵卖于屠市，足供一日酒肉资矣。」**
>
> *"If I tie up one ghost a night, spit on it to make it a sheep, and lead it to the butcher's market at dawn, that will cover a day's wine and meat."*

Notice the whole structure of a small enterprise in that sentence: a supply schedule (one per night), a production step (spit, to fix the shape), a distribution channel (the butcher's market at dawn), and a revenue target (one day's food and drink). He is not dreaming of wealth. He is not even dreaming of comfort. He has priced his ambition at **one day's wine and meat**, which tells you what his ordinary days were like, and it tells you that his plan is not a fantasy of escape but a plan to eat regularly.

Notice also that he has thought about the spit. He did not invent that step; he took it from the story, where it is a piece of magical technique, and he has folded it into his production process as though it were a curing method. This is what a literal mind does with a legend. Everyone else in the village heard a story about a clever man outwitting a demon. Jiang Sanmang heard a **recipe**.

Then he equips himself. **荷梃执绳** — a club over the shoulder, a rope in the hand. Four characters, and the funniest object in the tale is the rope. Song Dingbo did not need a rope; he used his shoulders and his nerve. But Jiang Sanmang is planning to bring one home, and a thing you carry home has to be tied, and you can only tie what has wrists. He has taken the metaphor of the story — *bound* — and gone out to buy the hardware.

The club is a separate joke. What, exactly, does one hit? The plan requires a ghost that can be stunned, trussed, and led on a halter at dawn through the streets of Xian County, past the yamen gate, to a butcher who will ask no questions about a sheep that arrived on a rope. Nobody in the story ever points out that the last man in the village to lead an animal to the butcher's block was leading a sheep that had always been a sheep. Jiang Sanmang does not require the plan to be coherent. He requires it to be **procedural**, and it is.

### IV. The Night Shift

**于是夜夜荷梃执绳，潜行墟墓间，如猎者之伺狐兔，竟不能遇。**

*So every night he shouldered his club, took his rope, and moved quietly among the ruined graves, the way a hunter waits for foxes and rabbits — and never met one.*

The simile is where the story quietly destroys him, and Ji Yun almost certainly knew it. A hunter waits where the game lives, at the hours the game keeps, because foxes and rabbits have habits: they come out at dusk, they follow the same runs through the same grass, they can be counted on. Jiang Sanmang has assumed the same of ghosts. He has assumed they have **habitat**. He has assumed they have a season. He has assumed that the graveyard at night is to a ghost what a warren is to a rabbit — that if you sit still in the right place, supply will arrive.

What he actually does, night after night, is patrol. In the dark, in the cold, in a county where the ground freezes in the eleventh month and the wind comes down off the Taihang with nothing in between, a grown man walks among the grave mounds with a stick, looking for inventory. The picture is absurd and also slightly terrible, in the way that all pictures of a person doing exactly what he said he would do are slightly terrible.

It is worth pausing on how strange this behaviour would have looked to his neighbours. A man loitering in a graveyard after dark, carrying a rope and a club, was not a harmless eccentric; he was the exact silhouette of a **grave robber**. Qing law treated the opening of graves with a savagery that reflects how common the crime was: the statute on 发冢 punished the mere uncovering of a coffin with penal servitude, and the sight of a corpse with death. Villages organised night watches against grave robbers. So Jiang Sanmang, in pursuit of an entirely innocent commercial plan, spent a month making himself indistinguishable from the one criminal his neighbours were most afraid of.[^yejin]

### V. The Respectable Haunted House

A month of empty graveyards should have ended the experiment. Instead he changes method.

**即素称有鬼之处，佯醉寝以诱致之，亦寂然无睹。**

*Then he went to the places that were always said to have ghosts, pretended to be drunk, and lay down there to lure them out. Nothing. Nothing at all.*

This is a real advance in technique, and it shows that he was not, in fact, a fool. He had been fishing in water where nobody had ever reported a fish. Now he goes where the fish are **reported** to be — the empty house at the end of the lane, the stretch of road where the carters would not camp, the temple courtyard after the lamps went out. Every county in China had a short list of such places. He works through the list.

And the bait he chooses is himself, feigning drunkenness: a man asleep, defenceless, exactly the kind of opportunity a malignant spirit is supposed to want. It is a genuinely intelligent trap, and it fails for the reason traps fail: the animal was never there.

There is a social dimension here that a modern reader can easily miss. In a village, courage was performed in public, and the standard performance was to spend a night in the haunted place and come out in the morning. Jiang Sanmang does not merely spend the night; he spends it **lying down, pretending to be drunk**, which is a more extravagant claim on the neighbours' attention than any of them had made. If he had come out in the morning saying he had seen something, the village would have had a new story and he would have had a new status. Instead he comes out with nothing, again and again, and the village learns something about him that is worse than cowardice: he is the man who is not worth haunting.

### VI. The Marsh Lights

Then, one evening, something happens.

**一夕，隔林见数磷火，踊跃奔赴；未至间，已星散去。懊恨而返。**

*One evening he saw several patches of marsh fire beyond the trees, and ran leaping toward them; before he got there they had scattered like sparks. He went home sick with disappointment.*

This is the only lyric moment in the story, and it is a lyric moment about a chemical reaction.

**磷火** — "phosphorus fire," what English calls will-o'-the-wisp, jack-o'-lantern, corpse candle — is the cold light that lifts off marshy ground and old burial earth on damp nights, when decaying organic matter releases phosphine and diphosphane, which ignite spontaneously on contact with air. In a county with shallow water tables and generations of graves, it was a common sight, and in the entire folk imagination of China it was the light of the dead: the vapour of the bones, walking.[^linhuo]

For one second, Jiang Sanmang's month of patience is rewarded. He sees lights where no lamps are, he does the arithmetic instantly — *lights in a graveyard at night, that is my inventory* — and he runs, **踊跃**, leaping, a word used of things that jump for joy. And the lights go out, or drift apart, or turn out to be what they always were: a few metres of swamp gas a long way off, dispersing in the wind.

Then four characters that are the emotional centre of the whole piece: **懊恨而返** — "he went back resentful and regretful." Not frightened. Not enlightened. *Annoyed.* He has been cheated by a physical phenomenon of a sale.

### VII. Giving Up

**如是月余，无所得，乃止。**

*This went on for more than a month. He got nothing. Then he stopped.*

The brevity is the joke. Ji Yun, who could stretch a scene when he wanted to, gives the ending of a month of nightly labour four characters plus a comma. No crisis, no conversion, no moment of terror that teaches him the fear of the Lord. He stops because **the inventory is zero**, and a business with no inventory closes. There is something magnificent about a man whose only reason for giving up on ghosts is that ghosts are bad suppliers.

### VIII. Ji Yun's Explanation

And then the author steps forward and tells us what it means, in one of the coldest sentences in Qing ghost literature:

> **盖鬼之侮人，恒乘人之畏。三莽确信鬼可缚，意中已视鬼蔑如矣，其气焰足以慑鬼，故鬼反避之也。**
>
> *For a ghost's way of insulting a man is always to take advantage of his fear. Sanmang was convinced that ghosts could be tied up; in his own mind he already held them as nothing at all. His blaze of vital force was enough to overawe a ghost — and so the ghosts avoided him instead.*

Everything in that sentence is a doctrine, and it is worth unpacking, because it is not the doctrine most modern readers expect.

First, **鬼之侮人** — the ghost's business is *insult*, not murder. Chinese ghosts in the zhiguai tradition are rarely killers. They frighten, they mislead, they sicken, they shame; their weapon is the effect they produce in the mind of the living. A ghost that nobody is afraid of has no weapon.

Second, **恒乘人之畏** — the mechanism is **timing**. A ghost strikes when fear has already opened the door. Fear is not the *result* of a haunting; in Ji Yun's account it is the *precondition*. The ghost is not a cause but an opportunist, and the opportunity is your own imagination.

Third, **蔑如** — "as nothing." Sanmang's advantage is not courage in the ordinary sense. Courage implies that you have correctly assessed a danger and decided to face it. Sanmang never assessed anything. He looked at the entire spirit world and saw a supplier who was late with a delivery. His fearlessness is a by-product of his stupidity, and Ji Yun knows it, and praises him anyway.

Fourth, **气焰** — the phrase translated above as "blaze of vital force." **气** is the vital vapour that Chinese medicine, philosophy and martial practice all agree a living body gives off; **焰** is flame. A man in high spirits literally radiates more of it. The idea is very old and very physical: a person whose 气 is full and settled is hard to approach, and a person whose 气 is scattered by fear is easy to approach — which is why the traditional advice for walking a night road alone was to keep your collar straight, your stride long, and your mind on something else. Ji Yun is not being metaphorical. He is describing a physiology of nerve.[^bupagui]

And so the final turn: **故鬼反避之也** — *and therefore the ghosts avoided him instead.* The hunter was the thing being avoided. He spent a month in the graveyards of Xian County, and the reason he caught nothing is that he was, from the ghost's point of view, the most dangerous object in the field.

### IX. The Long Tradition of Not Being Afraid

Ji Yun did not invent this idea, and he was not the first to find it funny. He was working inside one of the oldest continuous arguments in Chinese thought, and *Notes from the Thatched Study* — five books written in the author's seventies[^yuewei] — is stuffed with variations on it — a whole informal anthology of men and women who declined to be impressed.

The argument begins with **Wang Chong** (王充, 27–c. 97), the Han skeptic who devoted a chapter of the *Lun Heng* to the proposition that ghosts are produced by thinking about them: *"What the world calls a ghost is not the spirit of a dead man; it is what people's brooding and imagining brings into being."* Wang Chong's account of the mechanism is almost clinical — illness, exhaustion, grief and fear make the mind generate images, and the images are then seen. Seventeen centuries later Ji Yun is still running the same software: the ghost is real enough to be met, but the meeting is arranged by the person who is afraid.

The joke version of the same idea is older than Ji Yun's book and considerably ruder. In the *Ai Zi* collection attributed to Su Shi, a man crosses a ditch by pulling a god's statue out of a shrine and laying it across the water as a step; a second man, horrified at the sacrilege, puts the statue back, wipes it with his sleeve, and bows. The god then proposes to punish — the second man. Asked why, he explains that the first man does not believe, so there is nothing to work on. **「真是鬼怕恶人也」** — "so it is true that ghosts fear the wicked."

Ji Yun's own contribution is a whole gallery of the unimpressed. A scholar named Cao Zhuxu sleeps in a haunted study, watches a shape ooze through the door crack and unfold itself into a woman, then into a hanged ghost with its tongue out, and remarks that the hair is the same hair, merely messier, and the tongue the same tongue, merely longer; when the apparition takes its own head off and sets it on the desk, he observes that a head was no great threat and the absence of one is less. The ghost, out of material, vanishes. Ji Yun's comment on that story is the same as his comment on this one: the trick only works if you are watching it with the right face.

The tradition has a modern afterlife. In 1961 the scholar **He Qifang** compiled an anthology called *The Story of Not Fearing Ghosts* (《不怕鬼的故事》), drawing heavily on exactly this seam of Qing anecdote, and it was printed in quantities that make the *Notes from the Thatched Study* look like a limited edition. Read politically, as it was meant to be read, the book is an argument about intimidation in general: the ghost is whatever is standing in your way and counting on your nerve failing. Read literally, as Jiang Sanmang read Song Dingbo, it is a manual for a man with a rope.

### X. What the Story Is Actually About

Strip out the ghosts, and this is a story about **a market that only exists because people are afraid**.

In a village, a haunting is a shared asset. Everyone contributes to it: the man who saw the light, the woman who heard the crying, the carter who will not camp on that stretch of road, the grandmother who says her generation knew someone it happened to. The asset pays out in attention, in status, in the small authority of the person who knows which house is empty. It requires collective belief the way a currency requires collective confidence, and like a currency it collapses if enough people stop showing up.

Jiang Sanmang does not argue with the village. He does something more destructive: he tries to **monetise** the asset directly, and in the attempt he discovers the flaw. There is no supply. There never was. The ghost was never an object in the graveyard; it was a transaction between a place and a person's fear, and he had removed his half of the transaction before he left the house.

That is why the story has no climax. A ghost story normally ends with the apparition; this one ends with an inventory report. Ji Yun's irony is gentle and complete: he praises Sanmang's **气焰** in the language of moral philosophy — the vocabulary of 正气, the upright vapour that the *Mencius* says accumulates in a man who does right — and applies it to a man who was simply too literal to be scared. The virtue and the stupidity are the same trait, viewed from two angles, and Ji Yun declines to separate them.

What remains, after the joke, is the practical instruction, and it is the same instruction the book gives over and over: **the thing you are afraid of is doing most of its work inside you.** Ji Yun believed in ghosts; he says so repeatedly, and the *Notes* are full of them. He simply did not believe they could get in without your help. A man who cannot be impressed cannot be haunted, and the corollary is less comfortable: a man who can be impressed is, in some measure, already haunted, whether or not anything is there.

[^songdingbo]: The tale of Song Dingbo (宋定伯) is preserved in two early sources: the *Lieyi Zhuan* (《列异传》, "Records of the Strange"), a third-century collection attributed to Cao Pi and catalogued in the *Suishu* bibliography as three scrolls, and — in a slightly expanded form — scroll sixteen of Gan Bao's *Soushen Ji* (《搜神记》卷十六). Lu Xun gathered the surviving fragments of the *Lieyi Zhuan* into his *Guxiaoshuo Gouchen* (《古小说钩沉》). The version that Jiang Sanmang would have known was almost certainly not a book at all: Ji Yun's phrasing is **闻人说**, "he heard people tell," not "he read." The Song Dingbo story circulated for fifteen hundred years through storytellers, almanacs, household morality books and schoolroom primers, and it was one of the handful of ghost stories that a farmer in Zhili could be assumed to know by heart. The two details that survive every retelling are the ones that matter here: the **spit** (唾), a small dose of living human force used to fix a shapeshifter in its form, and the **price**, fifteen hundred cash. For a fuller treatment of the same tale on this site, see [The Young Man Who Sold a Ghost at the Market](../posts/the-young-man-who-sold-a-ghost).
[^jingcheng]: **Jingcheng** (景城) lies within **Xian County** (献县), Hejian Prefecture, in central Zhili — modern Hebei. It was the seat of the Ji clan, and Ji Yun's descriptions of its lanes, wells, temple fairs, fox-infested granaries and tiresome neighbours are the densest topographical writing in the *Notes*. The transmission formula at the head of the tale — **姚安公闻先曾祖润生公言** — identifies two earlier links in the chain: **Lord Yao'an** is Ji Yun's father, **Ji Rongshu** (纪容舒), who served as prefect of Yao'an in Yunnan and whose opinions are quoted on nearly every page of the book; **Runsheng** is the author's great-grandfather. Four generations of hearsay separate the event from the page, which is entirely typical: Ji Yun treated oral testimony the way a magistrate treated a deposition — worth recording, worth dating, worth naming the deponent.
[^yuewei]: The *Notes from the Thatched Study* (《阅微草堂笔记》) is not one book but five, written between 1789 and 1798, in the years after Ji Yun completed his decade as chief compiler of the *Siku Quanshu*: the *Luanyang Xiaoxia Lu* (滦阳消夏录, six scrolls), the *Rushi Wowen* (如是我闻, four), the *Huaxi Zazhi* (槐西杂志, four), the *Guwang Tingzhi* (姑妄听之, four) and the *Luanyang Xulu* (滦阳续录, six) — twenty-four scrolls and roughly twelve hundred separate entries, some four hundred thousand characters in all. Sheng Shiyan issued the first combined edition in 1800. "Luanyang" is Ji Yun's name for the imperial summer residence at Rehe, where he did his editorial work in the heat; the title means "notes taken at the thatched study while passing the summer." This tale sits in *Rushi Wowen* II, scroll eight — the second of the five books, and the one in which the old man's scepticism is most openly on display.
[^yejin]: The Qing legal code retained a night-watch statute (夜禁) inherited from the Ming, enforced by gate-closing, drums and village patrols, though in open country it functioned more as a social norm than as a police practice. The far more serious risk for a man wandering burial ground at night was being taken for a **grave robber**, and the statute on 发冢 was correspondingly brutal: the uncovering of a coffin drew penal servitude, and the exposure of a corpse drew death. Villages in Zhili organised their own night watches against exactly this crime, and the sight of a stranger with a club and a rope among the mounds would have been reported before morning. Ji Yun does not mention any of this — one of the pleasures of the *Notes* is watching a senior official write about village life with the legal apparatus silently switched off — but the joke only lands if you know what Sanmang looked like from the outside.
[^linhuo]: **磷火**, "phosphorus fire," is the cold flame that rises from marshy ground and old burial earth on warm, damp nights, when the decay of organic matter — including bone — releases phosphine and diphosphane, gases that ignite spontaneously in air. It drifts, flickers, and goes out when disturbed, which is precisely the behaviour that made it the most persuasive ghost in Chinese folklore. The identification with the dead was universal: the Cantonese called it 鬼火, "ghost fire," and the standard handbook explanation was that it was the vapour of the bones themselves, walking. By Ji Yun's century a few natural philosophers had begun to treat it as a chemical rather than a spiritual event, but the old reading survived in the countryside for another two hundred years, and the modern Chinese word for will-o'-the-wisp is still 鬼火. Sanmang's month of hunting ends, in effect, when he chases a gas.
[^bupagui]: The doctrine that a ghost's power is drawn from the fear it inspires has a long pedigree. **Wang Chong**'s *Lun Heng*, chapter 《订鬼》 ("On Ghosts"), states the case in almost laboratory terms: *"凡天地之间有鬼，非人死精神为之也，皆人思念存想之所致也"* — "What exists between heaven and earth as a ghost is not the spirit of a dead man; it is what thought and brooding bring about." Wang Chong adds that sickness, exhaustion and grief produce the visions, and that the frightened see most. The comic branch of the same tradition is the *Ai Zi* anecdote in which a god declines to punish the man who used his statue as a stepping stone, because the man does not believe, and proposes instead to punish the pious man who restored it — *"so it is true that ghosts fear the wicked."* Ji Yun's own contribution to the lineage is a cluster of tales scattered across all five books, of which the best known is that of the scholar **Cao Zhuxu** (曹竹虚), who watches a ghost run through its entire repertoire of horrors in a haunted study and critiques each one until it gives up and leaves. The tradition was still being mined in the twentieth century: **He Qifang**'s anthology *The Story of Not Fearing Ghosts* (《不怕鬼的故事》, 1961) drew heavily on Qing zhiguai, and was read at the time as an allegory about intimidation of every kind.

> **【原文】**
>
> > 姚安公闻先曾祖润生公言：景城有姜三莽者，勇而憨。一日，闻人说宋定伯卖鬼得钱事，大喜曰：「吾今乃知鬼可缚。如每夜缚一鬼，唾使变羊，晓而牵卖于屠市，足供一日酒肉资矣。」于是夜夜荷梃执绳，潜行墟墓间，如猎者之伺狐兔，竟不能遇。即素称有鬼之处，佯醉寝以诱致之，亦寂然无睹。一夕，隔林见数磷火，踊跃奔赴；未至间，已星散去。懊恨而返。如是月余，无所得，乃止。盖鬼之侮人，恒乘人之畏。三莽确信鬼可缚，意中已视鬼蔑如矣，其气焰足以慑鬼，故鬼反避之也。
>
> **【语词疏解】**
>
> > **憨**（亦作「戆」）：鲁直、迟钝而不通世故，非「愚笨」之贬义。纪昀用此字，兼含可笑与可敬。
> > **荷梃**：肩扛木棍。梃，直木，无刃，即所谓「白梃」，农家最贱之械。
> > **墟墓**：荒废之坟冢。「墟」本指废址，与「墓」连用，见其无人祭扫。
> > **蔑如**：视之若无。《说文》「蔑，劳目无精也」，引申为轻忽、不值一顾。
> > **气焰**：气之焰，谓人精神充盈外发之势。非今语「嚣张」之义。
> > **慑**：使之畏伏。此句主语为「其气焰」，谓三莽之气反而压住鬼，故曰「反避之」。
>
> **【白话对读】**
>
> > 姚安公听先曾祖润生公说：景城有个叫姜三莽的人，胆子大，性子憨直。一天听人说起宋定伯卖鬼得钱的故事，非常高兴地说：「我如今才知道鬼是可以捆起来的。要是每夜捆一个鬼，吐口唾沫让它变成羊，天亮牵到屠宰集市上卖了，就够一天的酒肉钱了。」于是夜夜扛着棍子拿着绳子，悄悄走在荒坟废冢之间，像猎人守候狐狸兔子一样——却始终没能遇上。又走到向来号称有鬼的地方，假装喝醉酒睡在那里，想把鬼引出来，也是静悄悄的什么也看不见。一天傍晚，隔着树林看见几点磷火，他连蹦带跳地奔过去；还没走到，已经像火星一样四散了。他懊恼悔恨地回了家。这样过了一个多月，什么也没得到，才停下来。原来鬼欺侮人，总是趁人害怕的时候下手。三莽确信鬼可以被捆住，心里早已把鬼看得无足轻重，他那股气焰足以压住鬼，所以鬼反倒躲着他。
>
> **【按语】** 此则与他篇不同处，在于「无事」。志怪之体，例必有遇：遇狐、遇鬼、遇怪，然后有惊、有斗、有报。而三莽独以「不遇」成篇——月余空手，一磷火而终。纪昀所写，其实不是鬼，而是一种「无效市场」：鬼之为物，须以人之「畏」为币；三莽不肯以畏易之，则虽有鬼亦无交易。故其结论「鬼反避之」，看似褒扬胆气，实为拆解：所谓鬼祟，多半是一场由被吓者自己出资的买卖。与卷一曹竹虚「鬼技穷」一则并读，可见纪昀一生志趣——他并不否认鬼之存在，只不肯承认鬼能白手起家。

<details class="historical-context">
<summary>🏛️ Historical Context · 历史背景</summary>

**Ji Yun, the most official man in Qing letters.** Ji Yun (纪昀, 1724–1805), courtesy name Xiaolan (晓岚), posthumous title Wenda (文达), was born in Xian County, Zhili, passed the metropolitan examination in 1754, and spent most of his adult life inside the Qing state. His great work was editorial rather than creative: from 1773 he was chief compiler of the **Siku Quanshu** (四库全书), the four-branch library into which the Qianlong emperor ordered every book in the empire gathered, copied, censored and catalogued — some 3,460 works, and a critical apparatus, the *Siku Quanshu Zongmu* (四库全书总目), in two hundred scrolls, that he largely wrote. He was also, in 1768, disgraced: implicated in leaking word of the salt-investigation to a relative, he was stripped of office and sent to **Urumqi** in Xinjiang for three years, an exile that gave the *Notes* their strangest material and their most convincing geography. He came back, was reinstated, rose to the highest literary offices in the land, and in his seventies began writing ghost stories.

**The book as a retirement project.** The *Notes from the Thatched Study* was written between 1789 and 1798, after the great library was finished, at the summer residence at Rehe and at his own house in Beijing. Ji Yun's preface is disarmingly modest: he had spent forty years reading and correcting other people's books, he says, and had no energy left for a masterpiece of his own; so when he remembered something he had heard, he wrote it down — **追寻前闻，忆及即书**. Twelve hundred entries came of it, and they are not composed like stories. They are composed like **case notes**: a source, a place, a date, an event, a comment. Sometimes the comment is a paragraph of jurisprudence, sometimes a paragraph on the physics of echoes, sometimes one line of dry sarcasm.

**Why it is not Liaozhai.** Readers have compared the two great Qing collections since both were in print. Pu Songling, in *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* (completed c. 1679, printed 1766), wrote **chuanqi** — full-dress literary tales with love plots, dialogue, poems and elaborate endings, in a style modelled on Tang narrative. Ji Yun wrote against exactly that. His declared preference was for the **zhiguai** manner of the Six Dynasties: plain, short, unadorned, "valuing substance and discarding ornament" (尚质黜华). He thought Pu Songling's embellishments were a form of lying — a tale told for its beauty rather than its truth. The result is a book of fragments in which a hundred and seventy characters can carry a whole philosophy, and in which the author's voice, dry and slightly amused, is the real protagonist. Lu Xun, in *A Brief History of Chinese Fiction*, gave him the verdict that has stuck: *"Ji Yun was a master of prose, had seen many rare books, and had a broad, unconstrained mind; so whenever he measured the conditions of ghosts and spirits and exposed the hidden places of the human heart, entrusting his own views to foxes and ghosts, his wit and fine phrases were often enough to make one smile. His narrative is calm and quietly elegant, full of natural delight — no one since has been able to take his place."*

**Xian County and the world of the Notes.** The settings are relentlessly local: the Ji family's own village of Jingcheng; the county town with its yamen, its granaries, its market days; the roads between Xian County and Hejian, and between Hejian and Beijing, where Ji Yun travelled for forty years. This is not the misty Jiangnan of the fox tales. It is the **North China plain**: wheat, wells, salt flats, cold winters, villages where the same twelve surnames have been arguing for two centuries, and where a rumour takes eight days to cross the county and twenty years to die. Ji Yun's ghosts live in that landscape and behave like its inhabitants — they keep grudges, they are curious about strangers, they are embarrassed, they leave when the conversation turns awkward.

**The economy of a small speculation.** Jiang Sanmang's plan is worth taking seriously as economics, because that is how the story works. In Qianlong-era Zhili, cash and silver circulated side by side at a fluctuating rate of roughly seven to nine hundred coppers to the tael; an agricultural labourer earned on the order of twenty to forty coppers a day plus food; a sheep at a county market cost several hundred to a thousand. Song Dingbo's fifteen hundred cash is therefore not a fortune — it is perhaps two months of a hired man's wages, which is exactly the right size for a legend that a poor man would remember. And Sanmang's own estimate is far more modest: one ghost equals **one day's wine and meat**. He has not priced the animal; he has priced his own appetite. The whole comedy of the tale sits in that gap — a man who has heard of a fortune and immediately converted it into lunch.

**Fear as a substance.** The doctrine Ji Yun states here — that ghosts feed on fright and are repelled by **气焰** — belongs to a much larger system of ideas about the body. In classical Chinese medicine and in the martial and meditative traditions, **气** is both breath and vitality, and its condition is legible from the outside: a frightened man's 气 scatters upward, his hands go cold, his voice rises; a settled man's 气 sinks and fills. The recommended countermeasures for a night road were all physical — straighten the collar, lengthen the stride, hum, do not look back — because they were understood to repair the 气 rather than to deny the ghost. Ji Yun's contribution is to notice that this advice, taken seriously, means the ghost's power is a function of the victim's posture; which means that a sufficiently dense and unimaginative man is not merely brave but **structurally immune**. Sanmang is not a hero. He is a wall.

**The afterlife of an anecdote.** This particular tale has had an unusually long second life as a schoolroom text. It appears in classical Chinese readers and examination anthologies across the Chinese-speaking world, usually with comprehension questions that ask the student to explain 蔑如, to punctuate 如是月余无所得乃止, and to write a short paragraph on what the story teaches about facing examinations without fear. The standard answer — that a thing you do not fear cannot harm you — is the answer the story invites, and it is not wrong. It is, however, a good deal more cheerful than what Ji Yun actually wrote, which is that the ghosts were not defeated, merely uninterested. The distinction matters. Ji Yun believed in ghosts and thought they were mostly petty; the schoolbook version believes in nothing and thinks the world is mostly kind. Only one of those positions is funny.

**Connection to other Cathay Tales.** This is the **fifteenth** tale on this site drawn from Ji Yun's collection, and it is deliberately placed beside its ancestor: [The Young Man Who Sold a Ghost at the Market](../posts/the-young-man-who-sold-a-ghost) (Tale 051, 宋定伯捉鬼) is the story Jiang Sanmang heard, and the whole plot of this one is what happens fifteen hundred years later when somebody believes it. Among the other Yuewei tales here, the closest in spirit are [The Pedant Whose Ghost Friend Saw No Light](../posts/the-pedant-whose-ghost-friend-saw-no-light) (Tale 024, 老学究遇鬼), where a ghost delivers a verdict on a scholar's learning, and [The Scholar Whose Ghost Asked the Ancients One Question](../posts/the-scholar-whose-ghost-asked-the-ancients-one-question) (Tale 041, 刘羽冲死读古书), where book-learning taken literally destroys the man who takes it literally — the same fault as Sanmang's, with a worse ending. For the collection at its most domestic and most frightening at once, see [The Ghost Who Sat Beneath the Lilac Tree](../posts/the-ghost-who-sat-beneath-the-lilac-tree) (Tale 075, 痴鬼望妻) and [The Beggar's Wife the Judge Refused to Honor](../posts/the-beggars-wife-the-judge-refused-to-honor) (Tale 080, 汤氏冤魂). And for the earliest stratum of the tradition Ji Yun is working in, [The Pig and the Old Man](../posts/the-pig-and-the-old-man) (Tale 001) shows the same author in his Six Dynasties manner: one incident, one verdict, no decoration.

## Translator's Note

The hardest word in this tale is **憨**. It has to carry both the ridicule and the admiration, because Ji Yun means both: this is a man whose intelligence is missing a floor, and whose missing floor is the reason nothing can get in. I have kept "bold and thick" in the first paragraph and let the rest of the story do the work.

The second hardest thing is the rope. In Chinese it is one character — 绳 — tucked into a four-character clause about carrying a club, and it is the funniest object in the book. A man who expects to bring a ghost home has already decided it has wrists. Everything after that clause is a man acting out a story he has misunderstood at exactly one point, and the misunderstanding is the plot.

What I did not expect, translating it, is how little the story needs its ghost. It is a comedy about supply and demand, and about the fact that the demand side is entirely other people's nerves. Ji Yun's last sentence is usually read as praise of courage. I think it is closer to an audit.

— *Wang Cai*
