# The Governor of Nanke / 南柯太守传

### *A dismissed officer falls asleep drunk under a locust tree and wakes twenty years later — having governed a province, buried a wife, lost a war, and been placed under house arrest inside an anthill*

*From **Taiping Guangji** (太平广记) juan 475, taken from the *Yiwén Lù* (异闻录) · By Li Gongzuo (李公佐, c. 770–c. 850)*

*Translated and annotated by Cathay Tales*

---

> His two friends were still in the room. One was soaking his feet in a basin. The wine was still standing in the cup by the east window, still clear. The sun had not yet gone down behind the western wall.
>
> Twenty years had passed.
>
> And when they went out into the yard with an axe and opened the hole in the locust tree, they found a palace: earthen walls the shape of battlements, a red terrace in the middle, two ants three inches long with white wings and crimson heads, and forty or fifty large ants standing guard around them while the rest of the colony — several bushels of it — kept its distance.
>
> That was the king. That was the capital. That was the whole of it.

## The Story

### I. The Man Under the Locust Tree

Chunyu Fen was from Dongping, and by the time he was thirty he had already been several kinds of man. In the Wu and Chu country he had been a *youxia* — one of those wandering retainers who carry a sword, keep their word, and are never quite employed. He drank. He was quick to take offence on his own behalf. He did not attend to small proprieties. He had inherited a great deal of property and spent it keeping open house for violent men, which in the Yangtze delta of the late eighth century was less a vice than a career path.

He had also, for a while, been something more respectable: he had entered the Huainan garrison as a subordinate general on the strength of his martial skills. It ended the way it always ended. He got drunk and said something to his commanding officer that could not be taken back, was cashiered, and went home to drink full time.

His house stood ten *li* east of Guangling — Yangzhou, the richest city in the empire, the southern terminus of the Grand Canal, a place where the salt monopoly, the transit trade and the provincial government had between them produced the densest concentration of money in China. And south of the house stood an old locust tree, enormous, its branches dense and its shade covering several *mu* of ground. Chunyu Fen drank under it every day with whatever gang of bravos he had assembled that week.

In the ninth month of the seventh year of Zhenyuan — the autumn of 791 — he made himself ill with drink. Two friends carried him indoors, laid him on a couch in the eastern wing, and told him to sleep; they would feed the horses and wash their feet, they said, and go once he had recovered a little.

He took off his headcloth, put his head on the pillow, and began to drift.

### II. The Two Men in Purple

Two messengers in purple robes appeared and knelt to him.

"The King of Huai'an has sent us to convey his command," they said. "He invites you."

Chunyu Fen got up, straightened his clothes without quite deciding to, and followed them to the gate. A small carriage stood waiting, lacquered green, drawn by four horses, with seven or eight attendants. They helped him in. The carriage left by the main gate, turned toward the hole at the foot of the old locust, and the messengers drove straight into it.

He thought this very strange and did not dare ask about it. Then the landscape opened out. Mountains and rivers, the feel of the air, trees, roads — all of it subtly wrong, subtly *other*, unlike anything in the world he knew. They travelled some tens of *li*. Walled towns appeared, with battlements; traffic thickened on the road, carriages and riders and pedestrians without a break. The outriders ahead of him shouted the right of way with great severity, and people scrambled aside.

They entered a great city. Vermilion gates, storied towers, and above the gate a plaque in letters of gold: **THE GREAT KINGDOM OF HUAI'AN**.

The gatekeepers ran to bow. A rider came up and called out: "His Majesty, on account of the son-in-law's distant arrival, bids him rest first at the Donghua Hostel." They led the way.

A gate opened. Chunyu Fen descended and went in. Painted railings, carved pillars, rare trees and precious fruit set out below in the courtyard; tables and cushions, curtains and hangings, dishes and delicacies laid out above. He was delighted.

Then a voice: "The Right Minister is coming."

He went down the steps to receive him. A man in purple with an ivory tablet at his belt came forward at a brisk pace, and the courtesies between host and guest were performed to the full. The Right Minister said: "My lord, not disdaining our small and distant country, has sent for a gentleman of quality, wishing to entrust him with a marriage connection."

Chunyu Fen said: "A man of my poor and inferior person — how would I dare hope for such a thing?"

The Right Minister asked him to come along. They walked about a hundred paces and passed through a vermilion gate. Spears and halberds and battle-axes were ranged on either side; several hundred officers of the guard stood along the road and gave way. Among them Chunyu Fen noticed a man who had been his drinking companion all his life — Zhou Bian. He was pleased, and did not dare ask.

The Right Minister led him up into a broad hall. The guard was severe, like the residence of a sovereign. A man sat in the place of honour, tall and dignified, dressed in white silk and wearing a cap ornamented with red flowers. Chunyu Fen trembled and did not dare raise his eyes. The attendants told him to bow.

The King said: "We have received the command of your honoured father. He did not disdain our small country, and consented that our second daughter, Yaofang, should serve you as wife."

Chunyu Fen only lay prostrate. He could not speak.

The King said: "For now, go to the guest house. The ceremonies will follow."

The Right Minister returned with him to the hostel. And Chunyu Fen began to think. His father had been a general on the frontier, had been lost among the northern tribes, and no one knew whether he lived. Had his father opened communications with the northern court? Was that where this came from? He was thoroughly confused and could not work out the reason for any of it.

### III. The Wedding

That evening the sheep and geese and silks, the ceremonial equipage, the music and strings, the dishes and candles, the carriages and gifts were all in readiness. There were numbers of women — one called Aunt Huayang, one Aunt Qingxi, one the Upper Immortal, one the Lower Immortal, and several more of that sort — each with a retinue of a thousand, wearing kingfisher-feather phoenix crowns and jackets of golden cloud-brocade, with ornaments of coloured jade and gold filigree, so bright that the eye could not rest on them. They sported and laughed and came and went through his gate, competing to make a fool of Master Chunyu. Their bearing was bewitching, their talk quick and gorgeous, and he could not answer any of it.

One of them said to him: "On the last Shangsi festival, I went with Lady Lingzhi to Chanzhi Temple and watched the Brahmā dance in the Tianzhu courtyard. We girls were sitting on the stone bench by the north window. You were a young man then and you had ridden over to see it. You came pushing in to be friendly, joking and teasing. My sister Qiongying and I tied a crimson kerchief and hung it on the bamboo. Don't you remember any of that?

"And on the sixteenth of the seventh month I was at Xiaogan Temple with the Shangzhen lady, hearing the Dharma Master Qixuan preach the Guanyin Sutra. I gave two gold phoenix hairpins at the end of the sermon; she gave a rhinoceros-horn box. You were in the congregation, and you asked the master to let you look at them, and you praised them over and over, and marvelled a long while. Then you turned to us and said: *'Neither these people nor these things are of this world.'* We asked where you were from and what your village was, and you did not answer. You looked at us with such longing, unable to look away. Surely you have not forgotten that?"

Chunyu Fen said: "I have kept it in my heart. When could I have forgotten?"

The women said: "We never thought we would be kin to you today."

Three men came forward then, imposing in cap and sash, and bowed: "We have been commanded to act as the groom's attendants." One of them was an old acquaintance, and Chunyu Fen pointed: "Are you not Tian Zihua of Fengyi?" Tian said he was. Chunyu Fen went to him and held his hand and they talked of old times for a long while. He asked how Tian came to be here. Tian said: "I was travelling without purpose and came to the notice of the Right Minister, Duke Duan of Wucheng. I have lodged with him since."

Chunyu Fen asked: "Zhou Bian is here. Did you know?"

Tian said: "Master Zhou is a man of consequence. He holds the office of Sili and his power is very great. I have several times enjoyed his protection."

They were laughing together when word came: "The groom may proceed." The three brought sword, girdle, cap and robes and dressed him. Tian said: "I never thought to see such a ceremony today. Do not forget me."

There were several dozen immortal maids playing unfamiliar music, winding and clear, the melodies sad — nothing that is heard among men. Several dozen more carried candles before him. On either side stood screens of gold and kingfisher feather, jewelled and pierced, unbroken for *li* together. He sat upright in the carriage, his heart unsettled and adrift, and Tian Zihua kept joking with him to steady him. The aunts and sisters rode past in phoenix-winged chairs. At a gate called the Xiuyi Palace they made him descend and bow and yield place and go up and come down, all exactly as among men. The screens were drawn back and the fans taken away, and there was a girl — Princess Jinzhi, they called her, "Golden Branch" — fourteen or fifteen years old, grave and still as a spirit.

The business of the marriage was conducted plainly enough.

From that day his affection and hers grew daily, and his glory grew with it. His carriages and dress, his excursions and feasts and attendants, ranked just below the King's. The King ordered him to take the guard and hunt on Spirit-Turtle Mountain west of the capital. The peaks were steep and fine, the marshlands broad, the woods thick, and every kind of bird and beast was kept there. The hunt was a great success and they came back at nightfall.

### IV. A Letter to the North

Some days later he said to the King: "On the day your Majesty spoke of our marriage, you said it was by my father's command. My father was serving under a frontier general, was defeated, and was lost among the northern tribes. There has been no letter these seventeen or eighteen years. Since your Majesty knows where he is, I beg to be allowed to go and pay my respects."

The King said quickly: "My good father-in-law holds a post in the north and the letters never stop. You need only write, and he will know of it. There is no need to go."

He had his wife prepare gifts, and sent them with the letter. Some nights later the answer came. Chunyu Fen examined the writing: it was all in his father's hand as he had written in life. The letter remembered him and instructed him, with all the tenderness and detail of former years. It asked after his relatives, living and dead, and after the rise and ruin of the old neighbourhood. Then it said that the roads were long and the wind and dust cut them off; the words were bitter, the tone grieving. And it would not permit him to come. It said: *in the year dingchou we shall meet.*

Chunyu Fen held the letter and wept and could not master himself.

### V. Twenty Years in Nanke

Another day his wife said to him: "Do you not think of holding office?"

He said: "I am a loose fellow. I have never studied administration."

She said: "Only take it. I will help you." And she spoke to the King.

Some days later the King said to him: "Our administration in Nanke is in disorder and the governor has been dismissed. We wish to borrow your ability. You may stoop to it — and our daughter will go with you."

Chunyu Fen accepted gravely.

The King had the officers prepare a governor's equipage, and brought out gold and jade, brocade and silk, chests and boxes, servants and concubines, carriages and horses, drawn up in the broad avenue to speed the princess on her way. Chunyu Fen, who in his youth had been a wanderer and had never dared hope for any of this, was delighted. He submitted a memorial:

> Your subject is a superfluous son of a military house and has never had any accomplishment. To be given a great charge by mistake is certainly to ruin the dynasty's statutes. I grieve that, like a man carrying a load beyond his strength, I shall overturn the cauldron.
>
> I wish to seek out worthy men widely, to make up for what I lack. I observe that the Sili, Zhou Bian of Yingchuan, is loyal and upright, firm and straight, holds the law without turning aside, and has the capacity of a counsellor. The retired scholar Tian Zihua of Fengyi is clear-minded and cautious, understands change, and comprehends the source of government and transformation. These two have been my friends for ten years and I know their abilities fully. Public affairs may be entrusted to them. I ask that Zhou be appointed director of justice in Nanke, and Tian director of agriculture, so that my administration may be known and the statutes not thrown into confusion.

The King granted it in full.

That evening the King and his consort gave a farewell feast south of the capital. The King said: "Nanke is a great province of this kingdom, its soil rich, its people proud and numerous. Nothing but benevolent government will hold it. And you have Zhou and Tian to assist you. Strive, and answer the hopes of the state."

The consort warned the princess: "Master Chunyu is by nature stubborn and fond of wine, and he is young. The way of a wife is to be gentle and yielding. Serve him well and I shall have no worries."

Nanke was not far, but morning and evening would be divided; and at this parting, how could they not wet their kerchiefs?

They bowed their heads and went south, mounted the carriage and gathered the riders, laughing and talking, and reached the province after several nights.

The province's officials, monks and priests, and elders, with music and carriages, guards and bells, all came out to welcome them. The crowds pressed and the drums and bells never stopped. For a dozen *li* they could see battlements and terraces, the air thick and fine over them. They entered the great gate, which carried a placard in gold letters: **THE CITY OF NANKE**. Vermilion carriages, ranked doors, deep and severe.

He descended. He inquired into customs. He treated the sick. He gave the administration to Zhou and Tian, and the province was greatly ordered.

He was governor for twenty years. The teaching of the province spread wide. The people sang of him, raised steles to his virtue, and built a shrine with his image in it while he was still alive. The King thought highly of him, gave him a fief and a title, and his rank stood with the highest ministers. Zhou and Tian both became known for their government and were promoted in turn. He had five sons and two daughters: the sons were given office by hereditary privilege, the daughters married into the royal clan. Such glory and prominence — in that time, in that place, nothing compared.

### VI. The War with Tanluo

In that year the kingdom of Tanluo came to attack the province. The King ordered Chunyu Fen to drill officers and train troops and go out against them. He memorialised that Zhou Bian should take thirty thousand men and hold the enemy at the city of Yaotai.

Zhou Bian was brave and impetuous, advanced too far, and the army was destroyed. He escaped alone, stripped, at night, and came back to the city on a single horse. The enemy collected the baggage and armour and went home.

Chunyu Fen put Zhou Bian in prison and asked for punishment on his own account. The King pardoned them both.

That month, Zhou Bian died of a carbuncle on the back. Within ten days the princess fell ill and died too.

Chunyu Fen asked to be relieved of the province so that he could escort the coffin to the capital. The King consented, and made Tian Zihua acting governor of Nanke. Chunyu Fen set out in mourning, the cortege moving slowly, men and women wailing, officials and commoners laying out offerings, crowds clinging to the shafts of the hearse and blocking the road — more than could be counted — until they reached the capital. The King and his consort, in white, wept in the suburbs as the hearse came up. The princess was given the posthumous name Shunyi, "Compliant and Right," and buried with full insignia and music on Panlong Ridge, ten *li* east of the capital. That month the son of the late Sili also brought his father's coffin to the capital.

### VII. The Omen

Chunyu Fen had held a frontier province for a long time and had made friends throughout the kingdom; there was no great house or powerful clan with which he was not on good terms. Since coming back from the province he went out and stayed in without pattern, and his guests and followers grew overbearing, and his power and his favours grew daily.

The King began to fear him.

Then a man of the kingdom submitted a memorial: *The dark patterns of the sky show a visitation. The state faces a great terror. The capital will be moved, the ancestral temple will collapse. The quarrel will rise from another clan; the trouble lies within the screen wall.*

The talk of the time took the omens as answering to Chunyu Fen's extravagance and presumption. His guards were taken from him. He was forbidden to go out or to keep company. He was placed in his own house.

He had held a province for many years and had never had a failure of government, and now slander and resentment had turned the King against him; he was sullen and unhappy. The King knew it. He sent word:

"We have been kin for more than twenty years. Unhappily our younger daughter died young and could not grow old with you, and that is grief indeed. The consort keeps the grandchildren and will raise them herself."

And then: "You have been away from home a long while. Go back to your own village for a time, and see your kin. The grandchildren stay here; do not fret for them. In three years we shall send for you."

Chunyu Fen said: "But this is my home. Where else would I go?"

The King laughed and said: "You are a man of the human world. Your home is not here."

### VIII. The Return

Chunyu Fen suddenly felt as though he had been in a heavy sleep, and was muddled for a long while, and only then remembered what had gone before. He wept and begged to be allowed to return.

The King looked at his attendants to see him out. Chunyu Fen bowed twice and left.

The two purple messengers were with him again. Outside the great gate the carriage he was to ride was a poor one, and there was not a single attendant, driver or servant. He was astonished and grieved.

He got in and went a few *li*, and came out through a great city gate, and it was just as though he were going east as he had come that year — mountains and rivers, plains and wilds, all as before. The two messengers had no dignity about them at all now, and he grew more and more despondent.

He asked them: "When will we reach Guangling?"

The two messengers sang on as if nothing had been asked. After a long while they answered: "In a little while."

Then they came out through a hole, and he saw the lanes of his own village, unchanged. He wept silently, and the tears came without his willing them.

The messengers helped him down from the carriage and he went in at his own gate and up his own steps — and saw his own body lying on the couch in the eastern wing.

He was terrified and did not dare approach. The messengers called his name aloud, several times, and he woke.

His servant was sweeping the courtyard with a broom. His two friends were soaking their feet on the couch. The slanting sun had not yet gone behind the western wall. The wine was still clear in the cup by the east window.

In the space of a dream, he had passed a lifetime.

### IX. The Excavation

He sighed and marvelled and called the two friends and told them everything. They were frightened. They went out with him to look for the hole under the locust.

He pointed. "That is where I went in, in the dream."

The two friends thought it must be the work of a fox spirit or a tree goblin, and had a servant take an axe and cut the swellings of the root and break off the dead shoots, hunting for the source of the hole.

A little to one side, about a *zhang* across, there was a great cavity, the root hollow and open, big enough to hold a couch. Above it, piled earth formed the shapes of walls, terraces and halls. Several bushels of ants were gathered there in hiding. In the middle was a small terrace, the colour of cinnabar, and on it two great ants — white wings, crimson heads, three inches long — with forty or fifty large ants on either side attending them, and no other ant daring to come near.

That was their king. That was the capital of Huai'an.

They opened another hole, going straight up the southern branch about four *zhang*: winding, square in the middle, with earthen walls and small towers, and a colony of ants living in it. That was the province of Nanke that he had governed.

Another hole, two *zhang* to the west: broad and hollow and crumbling, of strange uneven shape, and inside it a rotten turtle shell the size of a bushel, soaked by standing rain, with grass growing thick and rank over it and hiding the shell. That was Spirit-Turtle Mountain, where he had hunted.

Another, a little over a *zhang* to the east: old roots coiled and bent like dragons and snakes, and in the middle a small mound of earth a foot high. That was the tomb on Panlong Ridge where he had buried his wife.

He thought back over what had happened, and sighed from the bottom of his heart, and checked the traces one by one, and they all matched the dream. He would not let the two friends destroy them, and had them filled and blocked as before.

That night there was a violent storm of wind and rain. In the morning they looked at the hole: the whole colony was gone, and no one knew where.

So the earlier words — *the state faces a great terror, the capital will be moved* — had been verified.

He thought then of the invasion by Tanluo, and asked the two friends to look for traces outside. A *li* east of the house there was an old dried-up ravine, and beside it a great sandalwood tree, tangled and woven over with vines so that the sky could not be seen through it, and beside that a small hole with another colony of ants hidden in it.

The kingdom of Tanluo. Could it be anything else?

### X. What Happened to the Others

At that time his drinking companions Zhou Bian and Tian Zihua were both living in Luhe county, and he had not seen them for ten days. He sent a servant galloping to inquire after them.

Zhou Bian had died of a sudden illness. Tian Zihua was ill in bed.

Then Chunyu Fen understood the emptiness of Nanke, and awoke to the swiftness of the human world. He settled his heart in the Daoist gate and gave up wine and women.

Three years later, in the year *dingchou*, he too died at home, aged forty-seven — exactly the limit appointed beforehand.

In the autumn, the eighth month of the eighteenth year of Zhenyuan, I, Gongzuo, travelling from Wu to Luo, moored for a time on the Huai. I happened to meet Master Chunyu Fen and questioned him about what he had left behind. I went over it again and again; the matter is all taken from fact. I have written it out as a tale for the benefit of the curious. Though it deals with spirits and speaks of the strange, and the business is not according to the classics, yet those who steal office and live off it may hope to take warning from it. And you gentlemen of later days: be pleased to regard Nanke as an accident, and do not pride yourselves on name and place between heaven and earth.

The former army secretary of Huazhou, Li Zhao, appended this encomium:

> Rank at the very top of the scale of emolument, power that leans over the whole capital — to a man who sees through it, what is it but a swarm of ants?

## Notes

**The locust tree is not a random tree.** Of all the trees in China, the locust (*huái*, 槐) is the one that means *high office*. The *Rites of Zhou* places three locusts before the court, facing the throne, and assigns them to the Three Dukes; the nine jujubes beside them belong to the nine ministers. From that single line of ritual text comes a whole vocabulary: *sānhuái* (三槐) for the three highest offices in the land, *huáijí* (槐棘) for the seats of the great, and the custom — still alive in the Ming and Qing — of planting locusts at the gate of a house whose sons were expected to rise. Li Gongzuo chose his tree with a draftsman's precision. And then he named his hero Chunyu **Fen** (棼), and *fén* means a roof-beam: the timber that holds a great house up. A man named Beam falls asleep under the tree of the Three Dukes, is made a duke inside it, and wakes up to find his dukedom was a cavity in its root. The pun is not decoration. It is the argument.[^locust]

**The ants are the point, and the ants are an old joke.** Ants had been a miniature of human society in Chinese writing long before the Tang: they have their own kings, their own armies, their own envoys, and — in the *Book of the Southern Qi* — their own walled cities, reported by a man who claimed to have visited one in a dream. What Li Gongzuo did was to give the joke a full civil service. His anthill has a palace, a Right Minister, a Sili, a director of agriculture, a frontier province, a vassal enemy, a system of posthumous titles and a memorial culture of omens and self-criticism. Everything that makes a state a state is there, at a scale of one inch. The Chinese language took the lesson and kept it: **南柯一梦**, "one dream of the southern branch," is the ordinary modern way of saying that a great ambition came to nothing, and it is used by people who have never read a line of the tale.[^ant]

**What the omen actually was.** This is the finest piece of engineering in the story, and it is easy to miss. The memorial that destroys Chunyu Fen's career says that the sky has shown a visitation, that the capital will be moved, that the ancestral temple will fall, that the trouble will come from another clan and lie *within the screen wall*. Every reader of the Tang would have heard this as political astrology — as a coded accusation that the son-in-law had grown too strong. And every reader has just been told what it really was: a **weather report**. That night the storm came, and in the morning the colony had moved. *The capital will be moved* was true. It was a rain warning issued to an ant heap, translated by court astrologers into the language of state. Li Gongzuo lets you see both readings at once, and the horror is that they are the same sentence.[^omen]

**The political reading, which the Tang reader would not have needed a footnote for.** A man is posted to a frontier province. He holds it for twenty years. He builds a network among the great families, is given a fief and a title, and his rank stands with the highest ministers. He is then recalled to the capital, kept at loose ends, and — the moment a memorial raises the spectre of disloyalty — his guards are taken away, his movements are restricted, and he is confined to his own residence until a polite formula is found for sending him home. That is not a fantasy career. That is the standard life-cycle of the Tang **provincial military governor**, the *fanzhen*, the institution that had nearly ended the dynasty in the An Lushan rebellion forty years before this story was written and that every government afterwards treated as a disease to be managed. Li Gongzuo's anthill is a court; his Nanke is a province; and his hero's crime is exactly the crime the Tang state feared most: being loved in a place for too long.[^political]

**"But this is my home. Where else would I go?"** This is the line the tale turns on, and it is worth noticing that it is a joke at the expense of the reader. Chunyu Fen has spent twenty years in Nanke. He has a wife buried there, children living there, a stele with his name on it, a shrine with his image in it. He has been, by every measure his world recognises, a success. And he has forgotten — completely, sincerely, without any sense of loss — that he was ever anywhere else. When the King laughs and says *you are a man of the human world, your home is not here*, the laugh is the sound of a man being told that his entire life has been a posting. The dream does not end when he wakes up. It ends when someone tells him he was a guest.[^home]

**The messengers' answer.** When he asks how far it is to Guangling — his own house, ten *li* from where he is standing — the two purple messengers, who once knelt to him and drove him into a tree in a four-horse carriage, *sing on as if nothing had been asked*, and eventually say: *in a little while*. The distance has become unanswerable because he no longer has the rank to make it answerable. In the space of one line the ceremonial of the empire's most formal address collapses into two bored men humming on a cart. That is how power ends in this story: not with a sentence, but with a change in the quality of other people's attention.[^ending]

**The double ending.** Li Gongzuo closes with two voices, and they do not agree. His own is a moralist's: he offers the tale as a warning to men who hold office without deserving it. Li Zhao's encomium is a philosopher's: rank and power, seen by a man who understands, are a swarm of ants. But the story itself has already said something harder than either. Chunyu Fen was a *good* governor. The province was well ordered, the people sang of him, and they built his shrine while he was alive. His ruin came from an omen that was in fact a rainstorm, and his consolation was to be told that his twenty years of competence had occurred in a hole in a root. The tale is usually read as a warning against ambition. It works better as a warning against the *scale* on which ambition is judged — because if Nanke was an anthill, the reader is left holding the obvious question about the capital he is standing in.[^double]

东平淳于棼，吴楚游侠之士，嗜酒使气，不守细行，累巨产，养豪客。曾以武艺补淮南军裨将，因使酒忤帅，斥逐落魄，纵诞饮酒为事。家住广陵郡东十里，所居宅南有大古槐一株，枝干修密，清阴数亩，淳于生日与群豪大饮其下。唐贞元七年九月，因沈醉致疾，时二友人于坐扶生归家，卧于堂东庑之下。二友谓生曰：“子其寝矣，余将秣马濯足，俟子小愈而去。”生解巾就枕，昏然忽忽，仿佛若梦。见二紫衣使者，跪拜生曰：“槐安国王遣小臣致命奉邀。”生不觉下榻整衣，随二使至门。见青油小车，驾以四牡，左右从者七八，扶生上车，出大户，指古槐穴而去，使者即驱入穴中。生意颇甚异之，不敢致问。忽见山川风候，草木道路，与人世甚殊。前行数十里，有郛郭城堞，车舆人物，不绝于路。生左右传车者传呼甚严，行者亦争辟于左右。又入大城，朱门重楼，楼上有金书，题曰《大槐安国》。执门者趋拜奔走，旋有一骑传呼曰：“王以驸马远降，令且息东华馆。”因前导而去。俄见一门洞开，生降车而入。彩槛雕楹，华木珍果，列植于庭下；几案茵褥，帘帏肴膳，陈设于庭上。生心甚自悦。复有呼曰：“右相且至。”生降阶祗奉。有一人紫衣象简前趋，宾主之仪敬尽焉。右相曰：“寡君不以弊国远僻，奉迎君子，托以姻亲。”生曰：“某以贱劣之躯，岂敢是望。”右相因请生同诣其所。行可百步，入朱门，矛戟斧钺，布列左右，军吏数百，辟易道侧。生有平生酒徒周弁者，亦趋其中，生私心悦之，不敢前问。右相引生升广殿，御卫严肃，若至尊之所。见一人长大端严，居正位，衣素练服，簪朱华冠。生战栗，不敢仰视。左右侍者令生拜，王曰：“前奉贤尊命，不弃小国，许令次女瑶芳奉事君子。”生但俯伏而已，不敢致词。王曰：“且就宾宇，续造仪式。”有旨，右相亦与生偕还馆舍。生思念之，意以为父在边将，因没虏中，不知存亡。将谓父北蕃交通，而致兹事，心甚迷惑，不知其由。是夕，羔雁币帛，威容仪度，妓乐丝竹，肴膳灯烛，车骑礼物之用，无不咸备。有群女，或称华阳姑，或称青溪姑，或称上仙子，或称下仙子，若是者数辈，皆侍从数千，冠翠凤冠，衣金霞帔，采碧金钿，目不可视。遨游戏乐，往来其门，争以淳于郎为戏弄。风态妖丽，言词巧艳，生莫能对。复有一女谓生曰：“昨上巳日，吾从灵芝夫人过禅智寺，于天竹院观右延舞婆罗门，吾与诸女坐北牖石榻上。时君少年，亦解骑来看，君独强来亲洽，言调笑谑。吾与穷英妹结绛巾，挂于竹枝上，君独不忆念之乎？又七月十六日，吾于孝感寺侍上真子，听契玄法师讲观音经。吾于讲下舍金凤钗两只，上真子舍水犀合子一枚，时君亦讲筵中，于师处请钗合视之，赏叹再三，嗟异良久。顾余辈曰：‘人之与物，皆非世间所有。’或问吾民，或访吾里，吾亦不答。情意恋恋，瞩盼不舍，君岂不思念之乎？”生曰：“中心藏之，何日忘之。”群女曰：“不意今日与君为眷属。”复有三人，冠带甚伟，前拜生曰：“奉命为驸马相者。”中一人，与生且故，生指曰：“子非冯翊田子华乎？”田曰：“然。”生前，执手叙旧久之。生谓曰：“子何以居此？”子华曰：“吾放游，获受知于右相武成侯段公，因以栖托。”生复问曰：“周弁在此，知之乎？”子华曰：“周生贵人也，职为司隶，权势甚盛，吾数蒙庇护。”言笑甚欢，俄传声曰：“驸马可进矣。”三子取剑佩冕服更衣之。子华曰：“不意今日获睹盛礼，无以相忘也。”有仙姬数十，奏诸异乐，婉转清亮，曲调凄悲，非人间之所闻听。有执烛引导者亦数十，左右见金翠步障，彩碧玲珑，不断数里。生端坐车中，心意恍惚，甚不自安，田子华数言笑以解之。向者群女姑娣，各乘凤翼辇，亦往来其间。至一门，号修仪宫，群仙姑姊，亦纷然在侧。令生降车辇拜，揖让升降，一如人间。撤障去扇，见一女子，云号金枝公主，年可十四五，俨若神仙。交欢之礼，颇亦明显。生自尔情义日洽，荣曜日盛，出入车服，游宴宾御，次于王者。王命生与群寮备武卫，大猎于国西灵龟山。山阜峻秀，川泽广远，林树丰茂，飞禽走兽，无不蓄之。师徒大获，竟夕而还。生因他日启王曰：“臣顷结好之日，大王云奉臣父之命。臣父顷佐边将，用兵失利，陷没胡中，尔来绝书信十七八岁矣。王既知所在，臣请一往拜觐。”王遽谓曰：“亲家翁职守北土，信问不绝，卿但具书状知闻，未用便去。”遂命妻致馈贺之礼，一以遣之。数夕还答，生验书本意，皆父平生之迹，书中忆念教诲，情意委屈，皆如昔年。复问生亲戚存亡，闾里兴废。复言路道乖远，风烟阻绝，词意悲苦，言语哀伤，又不令生来觐。云岁在丁丑，当与女相见。生捧书悲咽，情不自堪。他日，妻谓生曰：“子岂不思为政乎？”生曰：“我放荡，不习政事。”妻曰：“卿但为之，余当奉赞。”妻遂白于王。累日，谓生曰：“吾南柯政事不理，太守黜废，欲藉卿才，可曲屈之，便与小女同行。”生敦受教命。王遂敕有司备太守行李，因出金玉锦绣，箱奁仆妾车马列于广衢，以饯公主之行。生少游侠，曾不敢有望，至是甚悦。因上表曰：“臣将门余子，素无艺术。猥当大任，必败朝章。自悲负乘，坐致覆餗。今欲广求贤哲，以赞不逮。伏见司隶颍川周弁忠亮刚直，守法不回，有毗佐之器。处士冯翊田子华清慎通变，达政化之源。二人与臣有十年之旧，备知才用，可托政事。周请署南柯司宪，田请署司农，庶使臣政绩有闻，宪章不紊也。”王并依表以遣之。其夕，王与夫人饯于国南。王谓生曰：“南柯国之大郡，土地丰壤，人物豪盛，非惠政不能以治之，况有周田二赞，卿其勉之，以副国念。”夫人戒公主曰：“淳于郎性刚好酒，加之少年，为妇之道，贵乎柔顺，尔善事之，吾无忧矣。”南柯虽封境不遥，晨昏有间，今日暌别，宁不沾巾。生与妻拜首南去，登车拥骑，言笑甚欢，累夕达郡。郡有官吏僧道耆老，音乐车舆，武卫銮铃，争来迎奉。人物阗咽，钟鼓喧哗不绝。十数里，见雉堞台观，佳气郁郁。入大城门。门亦有大榜，题以金字，曰“南柯郡城”。是朱轩棨户，森然深邃。生下车，省风俗，疗病苦，政事委以周田，郡中大理。自守郡二十载，风化广被，百姓歌谣，建功德碑，立生祠宇。王甚重之，赐食邑锡爵，位居台辅。周田皆以政治著闻，递迁大位。生有五男二女，男以门荫授官，女亦娉于王族，荣耀显赫，一时之盛，代莫比之。是岁，有檀萝国者，来伐是郡。王命生练将训师以征之，乃表周弁将兵三万，以拒贼之众于瑶台城。弁刚勇轻进，师徒败绩，弁单骑裸身潜遁，夜归城。贼亦收辎重铠甲而还。生因囚弁以请罪，王并舍之。是月，司宪周弁疽发背卒。生妻公主遘疾，旬日又薨。生因请罢郡，护丧赴国，王许之，便以司农田子华行南柯太守事。生哀恸发引，威仪在途，男女叫号，人吏奠馔，攀辕遮道者，不可胜数，遂达于国。王与夫人素衣哭于郊，候灵舆之至。谥公主曰顺仪公主，备仪仗羽葆鼓吹，葬于国东十里盘龙冈。是月，故司宪子荣信亦护丧赴国。生久镇外藩，结好中国，贵门豪族，靡不是洽。自罢郡还国，出入无恒，交游宾从，威福日盛，王意疑惮之。时有国人上表云，玄象谪见，国有大恐，都邑迁徙，宗庙崩坏。衅起他族，事在萧墙。时议以生侈僭之应也，遂夺生侍卫，禁生游从，处之私第。生自恃守郡多年，曾无败政，流言怨悖，郁郁不乐。王亦知之，因命生曰：“姻亲二十余年，不幸小女夭枉，不得与君子偕老，良用痛伤。夫人因留孙自鞠育之。”又谓生曰：“卿离家多时，可暂归本里，一见亲族，诸孙留此，无以为念。后三年，当令迎生。”生曰：“此乃家矣，何更归焉？”王笑曰：“卿本人间，家非在此。”生忽若惛睡，瞢然久之，方乃发悟前事，遂流涕请还。王顾左右以送生，生再拜而去。复见前二紫衣使者从焉，至大户外，见所乘车甚劣，左右亲使御仆，遂无一人，心甚叹异。生上车行可数里，复出大城，宛是昔年东来之途，山川源野，依然如旧。所送二使者，甚无威势，生逾怏怏。生问使者曰：“广陵郡何时可到？”二使讴歌自若。久之乃答曰：“少顷即至。”俄出一穴，见本里闾巷，不改往日。潸然自悲，不觉流涕。二使者引生下车，入其门，升自阶，己身卧于堂东庑之下。生甚惊畏，不敢前近。二使因大呼生之姓名数声，生遂发寤如初，见家之僮仆，拥篲于庭，二客濯足于榻，斜日未隐于西垣，余樽尚湛于东牖。梦中倏忽，若度一世矣，生感念嗟叹，遂呼二客而语之，惊骇，因与生出外，寻槐下穴。生指曰：“此即梦中所惊入处。”二客将谓狐狸木媚之所为祟，遂命仆夫荷斤斧，断拥肿，折查枿，寻穴究源。旁可袤丈，有大穴，根洞然明朗，可容一榻，上有积土壤，以为城郭台殿之状，有蚁数斛，隐聚其中。中有小台，其色若丹，二大蚁处之，素翼朱首，长可三寸，左右大蚁数十辅之，诸蚁不敢近，此其王矣，即槐安国都也。又穷一穴，直上南枝可四丈，宛转方中，亦有土城小楼，群蚁亦处其中，即生所领南柯郡也。又一穴，西去二丈，磅礴空朽，嵌窞异状，中有一腐龟壳，大如斗，积雨浸润，小草丛生，繁茂翳荟，掩映振壳，即生所猎灵龟山也。又穷一穴，东去丈余，古根盘屈，若龙虺之状，中有小土壤，高尺余，即生所葬妻盘龙冈之墓也。追想前事，感叹于怀，披阅穷迹，皆符所梦。不欲二客坏之，遽令掩塞如旧。是夕，风雨暴发。旦视其穴，遂失群蚁，莫知所去。故先言国有大恐，都邑迁徙，此其验矣。复念檀萝征伐之事，又请二客访迹于外。宅东一里，有古涸涧，侧有大檀树一株，藤萝拥织，上不见日，旁有小穴，亦有群蚁隐聚其间，檀萝之国，岂非此耶！嗟乎！蚁之灵异，犹不可穷，况山藏木伏之大者所变化乎？时生酒徒周弁、田子华，并居六合县，不与生过从旬日矣，生遽遣家僮疾往候之。周生暴疾已逝，田子华亦寝疾于床。生感南柯之浮虚，悟人世之倏忽，遂栖心道门，绝弃酒色。后三年，岁在丁丑，亦终于家，时年四十七，将符宿契之限矣。公佐贞元十八年秋八月，自吴之洛，暂泊淮浦，偶觌淳于生棼，询访遗迹。翻复再三，事皆摭实，辄编录成传，以资好事。虽稽神语怪，事涉非经，而窃位著生，冀将为戒。后之君子，幸以南柯为偶然，无以名位骄于天壤间云。前华州参军李肇赞曰：“贵极禄位，权倾国都。达人视此，蚁聚何殊。”（出《异闻录》）

<details class="historical-context">
<summary>Historical context: the world behind the tale</summary>

**Guangling, 791.** Yangzhou in the late eighth century was the largest commercial city in China and the administrative capital of the Huainan circuit. It sat where the Grand Canal met the Yangtze, which meant that the grain tax of the entire southeast passed through it, and that the salt monopoly — the state's single most reliable source of cash after the An Lushan rebellion — was administered from it. A man with a house ten *li* east of the city wall, a private income, and a stable of armed retainers was not a rustic. He was a small provincial magnate in the richest province in the empire, which is precisely the sort of man who has the leisure to dream about a career.

**The dream landscape.** Li Gongzuo's otherworld is a bureaucracy, and so is his dream. That is the characteristic Tang move: the supernatural in *chuanqi* is not a place of mystery but a place of **administration**, staffed by officials with titles, reached by carriage, entered through a gate with a plaque on it, and governed by memorials. The tale is often called a Buddhist or Daoist parable of illusion. It is also, and more exactly, a piece of civil-service satire written by a man who spent his life in and around the provincial bureaucracy and knew what a career looked like from the inside: the probation, the appointment, the twenty years in one post, the network, the recall, the memorial, the house arrest.

**The two temples and the two festivals.** The women at the wedding tease Chunyu Fen with memories of specific Yangzhou occasions: the Shangsi festival (third day of the third month, the spring purification, when the whole city went out to the water) and the sixteenth of the seventh month. Chanzhi Temple, on the Shugang ridge north of the city, was one of the most famous monasteries in the southeast and a standard stop on any Yangzhou outing; the *Brahma dance* they watch there is the kind of Central Asian entertainment that reached China through the Sogdian trade and became a fixture of Tang urban festival culture. The detail that fixes the scene is the donation: a pair of gold phoenix hairpins and a rhinoceros-horn box, offered at the end of a sutra lecture, and then handed round among the congregation to be admired. That is not invented ritual. It is a description of what wealthy women did at a Tang temple on a preaching day, and it is delivered as evidence in a court of memory — *don't you remember?*

**The Tang dream-vision genre.** *Nanke* and Shen Jiji's *Record of the Pillow* (枕中记, the millet-dream of a lifetime of glory, on this site as [The Pillow That Dreamed a Lifetime](/posts/the-pillow-that-dreamed-a-lifetime)) are the twin founding documents of the Chinese dream tale, and they were written within a generation of each other. Both are about a man who is given a complete official career inside a few minutes of real time. Both end with the dreamer turning away from the world. The difference is the tone: Shen Jiji's Lü Weng is offered the dream as a *lesson* by a Daoist who is teaching him to want less; Chunyu Fen is offered his by an ant colony that never intended to teach him anything. One is a parable. The other is a machine.

**Afterlife.** The tale's reach is out of all proportion to its length. Tang poets used 南柯 as shorthand within a century. Song and Ming drama took it up; **Tang Xianzu** — the greatest playwright of the late Ming — made it the basis of *The Record of Nanke* (《南柯记》, 1600), one of the four plays he grouped as his *Four Dreams*, alongside *The Peony Pavilion*. The idiom entered Japanese and Korean literary Chinese. And in Yangzhou, a locust tree in a lane in the old city is still pointed out to visitors as *the* tree, which is a claim no one can test and no one bothers to dispute.

</details>

## Translator's Note

There is a particular kind of despair that only a successful man can feel, and Li Gongzuo located it eleven hundred years before anyone else got close. Chunyu Fen is not a failure. He governs well for twenty years, his people build him a shrine, his sons are placed, his daughters marry into the royal house — and none of it survives contact with a rainstorm. What makes the story unbearable is not that the glory was an illusion. It is that the glory was *real*, and the illusion was the scale.

Every translation of this tale has to decide what to do with the ending, where Li Gongzuo steps forward in his own voice, dates his own meeting with his own character, and assures us that the matter is all taken from fact. He is lying, of course, and he is lying in a way that was entirely conventional: the frame-narrator who certifies the impossible is one of the oldest devices in Chinese fiction. But the date he gives himself is 802, and the man he says he met had died in 797, and the arithmetic of the dream has never quite come out. I have left all of it standing. A story about a life that does not add up should not be given an edition that tidies it.

— *Wang Cai*

Li Gongzuo gave a man named Beam a dukedom inside the tree of the Three Dukes, and then took it away by cutting the roots with an axe.

[^ant]: The ant kingdom is one of the oldest microcosms in Chinese letters. Ants were credited with kings, armies, granaries and envoys; the *Nan Qi Shu* (《南齐书》) records a man who dreamed of entering an ant city with its own walls and government, and the *Youyang Zazu* and *Taiping Guangji* preserve a whole cluster of "ant kingdom" anecdotes, of which this is the longest and the only one that has never stopped being read. Li Gongzuo's innovation is not the idea of an insect society but the **completeness of its institutions**: his colony has a Right Minister with a fief (Duke Duan of Wucheng), a Sili, a director of agriculture, a frontier province with a governor's equipage, a vassal enemy state, a system of posthumous names, and an omen-and-remonstrance culture. The Chinese language absorbed the result as **南柯一梦** — "one dream of the southern branch" — today the ordinary phrase for an ambition that came to nothing, used by people who could not name the author.

[^omen]: 玄象谪见，国有大恐，都邑迁徙，宗庙崩坏。衅起他族，事在萧墙 — "the dark patterns show a visitation; the state faces a great terror; the capital will be moved; the ancestral temple will collapse; the quarrel will rise from another clan; the trouble lies within the screen wall." Every phrase is the standard vocabulary of Tang political astrology, and 事在萧墙 (literally "the affair is at the screen wall," i.e. inside the family compound) is a quotation from the *Analects*, where it describes trouble that comes from within a ruler's own household rather than from outside. Read politically, the memorial is an accusation that the son-in-law has become a threat from inside the family. Read literally — and the story has already told you how to read it literally — it is a statement that the colony is about to be flooded out and will relocate. The brilliance of the construction is that the astrologers are not wrong. They are simply reporting on a smaller state than they think.

[^political]: The institution in question is the *fanzhen* (藩镇), the provincial military command that emerged after the An Lushan rebellion of 755–763 and that the Tang court spent the next century trying to control. The pattern Li Gongzuo describes — long tenure in a frontier province, marriage into the ruling house, an independent network among the great clans, a fief and an honorific rank, then recall to the capital and confinement — is close enough to the standard career of a restive governor that Tang readers would have recognised the template immediately. The detail of the **guards being withdrawn** (夺生侍卫) is the crucial one: it is what the court did to a man it could no longer afford to leave alone but could not yet afford to execute. It should be said that Li Gongzuo never states the parallel, and the tale is not an allegory with a key. But the choice to make his hero's downfall turn on a *memorial about omens* rather than on any act of misgovernment is not an accident: in the Tang, that is how governors fell.

[^home]: 生曰："此乃家矣，何更归焉？" 王笑曰："卿本人间，家非在此。" The exchange is the shortest and most brutal in the tale, and its force depends on a fact the reader has been allowed to forget: Chunyu Fen has been living in a hole in a tree root for twenty years. He is not confused about geography. He is confused about *belonging*, which is the more permanent condition — and the King's laugh is the sound of a host reminding a guest how long he has stayed. Compare the ending of Shen Jiji's *Record of the Pillow*, where the dreamer's realisation is greeted with a moral; here there is no moral, only a laugh and a cart.

[^ending]: 二使讴歌自若，久之，乃答曰："少顷即至。" — "the two messengers sang on at their ease; after a long while they answered: 'In a little while.'" The verb 讴歌 (to sing, to hum a tune) is doing an enormous amount of work. On the outward journey these same two men **knelt** to him (跪拜) and drove him through the tree in a four-horse carriage with outriders shouting the road clear. On the return they hum. The ceremonial distance between a kneeling messenger and a humming one is the whole distance between a career and its aftermath, and Li Gongzuo covers it in five characters without a single comment.

[^double]: The frame contains a genuine chronological crux, which the standard editions print without resolving. The dream is dated to the ninth month of the seventh year of Zhenyuan (791); the King promises to send for him again "after three years" (后三年); the father's letter names the year *dingchou* (丁丑, = 797) as the date of their meeting; and the tale says he died "three years later, in the year *dingchou*, aged forty-seven." Seven ninety-one plus three is 794, not 797. Meanwhile Li Gongzuo dates his own encounter with Chunyu Fen to the eighth month of the eighteenth year of Zhenyuan (802) — five years after the man he interviewed had died. Some of this is scribal; some of it is the deliberate slipperiness of a frame-narrator who certifies what he has invented. It has also been read, more interestingly, as the tale quietly telling you that the *frame* is the dream and the anthill is the only solid ground. I have not emended any of it.
